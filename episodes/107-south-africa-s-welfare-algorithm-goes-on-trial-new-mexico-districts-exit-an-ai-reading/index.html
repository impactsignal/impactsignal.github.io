#107: Impact Signals #107 — South Africa's Welfare Algorithm Goes on Trial, New Mexico Districts Exit an AI Reading Mandate, Pakistan's First Provincial Disaster Policy

Top Stories

Sixteen Million Recipients Became 5.6 Million After Automation: South Africa's Top Appeals Court Hears the Algorithm Case on August 25

On August 25, South Africa's Supreme Court of Appeal hears the government's appeal in Institute for Economic Justice v. SASSA, the case that will decide whether the country's fully automated Social Relief of Distress grant survives. The SRD grant, launched in May 2020, pays R370 a month (about $23) to people with no other income; applications are digital-only, and eligibility is decided by algorithmic checks against bank records and government databases. The grant reached roughly 16 million people at launch, but after digitization the beneficiary count fell to 5.6 million by 2022, against an estimated 18.3 million people who meet the eligibility criteria. In January 2025 the High Court declared the regulations authorizing the automated verification unconstitutional, finding it unfairly excluded eligible applicants; the state appealed, and the ruling is suspended until the appeal is decided. In an analysis published today in The Conversation, Wits University's Mark Gaffley argues the system's income threshold sits almost 50 percent below the food poverty line, and that flawed data and opaque algorithmic processes have been quietly excluding the people the grant exists for. Daily Maverick and Open Secrets have separately documented the role of contracted commercial banks in the income-verification step. Why it matters: This is the first time a top appellate court anywhere in Africa will rule on whether a government may determine welfare eligibility by algorithm, and the outcome directly governs access to income support for millions of people. Whatever the court decides on August 25 will set the continent's leading precedent on automated decision-making in public services, and social-protection agencies elsewhere that are digitizing eligibility checks will be operating in its shadow.

Sources: The Conversation, Daily Maverick, Wits University

New Mexico Mandated an AI Reading Tutor for Every K-2 Classroom; Six School Systems Have Now Walked Out Over Student Voice Data

New Mexico requires every public and charter school to run Amira, an AI reading tool, as the statewide K-2 assessment plus a minimum of 30 minutes of AI tutoring per week. The software listens to children read aloud and flags errors, and those voice recordings help train the vendor's models. After parent backlash, the state Public Education Department reversed course on August 11 and allowed districts to opt out in favor of alternative assessments; Santa Fe, Los Alamos, Farmington, Roswell, and Clayton, plus Turquoise Trail Charter School, have done so. Los Alamos superintendent Jennifer Guy calls student voice recordings biometric data that should not be collected by a third-party system as a condition of a state-mandated test. The state pays about $2.7 million a year for Amira, $1.4 million of it for the assessment component. The counter-evidence is real: Albuquerque Public Schools reports its first and second graders posted their highest reading scores since at least 2021 while using the tool, and it stays in. Why it matters: This is the clearest live test of what happens when a state makes an AI system mandatory infrastructure for small children rather than an optional aid. The opt-out reversal establishes that biometric-privacy objections can force a state education agency to retreat from a signed statewide AI mandate, and the districts now running parallel non-AI assessments will generate the first comparative data on whether the tool's reading gains hold up.

Sources: GovTech, Education Week, Albuquerque Journal

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Ships Pakistan's First Provincial Disaster Risk Reduction Policy, Moving Money From Relief to Early Warning

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa launched Pakistan's first sub-national Disaster Risk Reduction Policy on August 17 at the Chief Minister's House in Peshawar, organized by the Provincial Disaster Management Authority and the Relief, Rehabilitation and Settlement Department, with support from Germany's Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development. KP is the first Pakistani province to take a DRR policy through its provincial cabinet. The stated shift is from reactive relief to anticipatory risk management: preparedness, early warning, and scientific risk assessment before disasters strike rather than compensation after. It lands in the province that carries much of Pakistan's glacial-flood exposure, and it is the governance layer that the country's expanding early-warning hardware, including the 286 glacial-flood warning systems announced for KP and Gilgit-Baltistan this week, will have to plug into. Why it matters: Pakistan's disaster losses have been governed nationally and managed reactively; a cabinet-approved provincial policy is the first structural commitment to fund risk reduction before the flood, and it creates the legal and budget frame that determines whether the new warning systems get maintained and acted on. It also gives Pakistan's other provinces a template that did not exist a week ago.

Sources: ProPakistani, The Express Tribune, TNN

Can an LLM Stand In for a Refugee Household Survey? A NeurIPS Competition Will Test "AI Simulacra" Against Sealed UNHCR and UNICEF Microdata

SimulacraBench, an official NeurIPS 2026 competition that publicly launched August 17, takes direct aim at one of the most consequential shortcuts emerging in humanitarian data work: using large language models as stand-ins for hard-to-reach populations, to pre-test survey instruments, impute non-response, and run subgroup what-if analyses. Field data collection is slow, expensive, and gappy, and the temptation to fill those gaps with model-generated answers is growing faster than the evidence that it works. The competition's design is what makes it credible: entries are validated against unreleased UNHCR, UNICEF, and World Bank microdata, so a model cannot have memorized the answer key. The development phase runs through October 30, 2026, with results presented at NeurIPS. Why it matters: Agencies are already under budget pressure to substitute model outputs for field data, and until now there has been no shared, held-out benchmark to say when that substitution is safe. The accuracy numbers that come out of this competition will become the reference point for whether synthetic respondents have any legitimate place in refugee and child-welfare statistics, and the sealed UN microdata design means the answer will be hard to argue with. Entries are open now at simulacrabench.org.

Sources: SimulacraBench, NeurIPS
