#165: Impact Signals #165 — North Carolina Tests AI on SNAP Recertification, Governments Pull Sites Offline Over an AI-Found PHP Flaw, Britain Funds a £100 Million AI Biosecurity Lab

AI for Impact Daily Briefing, October 24, 2026

Listen on: Spotify Apple Podcasts YouTube Substack RSS

Top Stories

North Carolina Will Test AI on SNAP Recertification as an $11 Million Round Funds 11 State Benefits Projects

The Public Benefit Innovation Fund, run by the Center for Civic Futures (a program of Renaissance Philanthropy), has awarded $11 million across 11 projects that apply AI to the machinery behind Medicaid, SNAP and unemployment insurance, StateScoop reported on October 23. North Carolina will test an AI-assisted SNAP recertification process, starting on synthetic data before touching real cases, and Stanford RegLab will work with Maryland and Colorado on unemployment insurance applications. Other projects flag SNAP cases likely to contain errors, connect corrections data with Medicaid and SNAP systems, and translate SNAP eligibility rules into machine-readable form. The fund's first round produced a Mississippi SNAP verification tool that cut the state's payment-error liability by $40 million, and New Jersey tools that helped deliver an estimated $19 million in benefits to more than 97,000 residents. Why it matters: H.R. 1 requires more frequent Medicaid eligibility checks and new work requirements, and shifts more SNAP administrative costs and payment-error liability onto states, so the paperwork these tools target is growing and its error rate now carries a direct state budget cost. Close to half of applicants proposed chatbots; the funder backed recertification, error flagging and rules-as-code instead, and fund staff named procurement as a blocker. Grants run six months to two years, and North Carolina's first results will come from synthetic data, before any household's benefits depend on the tool.

Sources: StateScoop

AI Agents Found a Flaw Under 70 Percent of the Web, and Governments Took Their Own Sites Offline to Patch It

Two critical vulnerabilities in PHP were disclosed on October 21 after AI agents helped find them, the Associated Press and Reuters reported. OpenAI said it led a coalition of researchers and security providers that worked on PHP over the previous two months and that AI appeared to find the flaws before attackers did. The FBI's cyber division warned the bugs could allow remote code execution and recommended taking sites offline if they could not be updated at once; the PHP Foundation released patches that evening. Government websites went dark in France, Denmark, Finland and the Netherlands (including the Dutch cyber security agency's own site), the UK said some "limited" services might be offline, Osaka prefecture's machine translation service went down, and the Wikimedia Foundation took a number of systems down as a precaution. Why it matters: This is the first time AI-driven vulnerability discovery has visibly taken public services offline at once across several countries, and the outages were deliberate defensive shutdowns. The cost fell on government portals, translation services and Wikimedia, the systems people use to reach public information. Cloudflare's chief executive reported "millions" of exploit attempts within hours, the AP reported no confirmed breaches, and Wordfence warned that automatic updates would not apply the fix, so any site not patched by hand remains exposed.

Sources: Reuters via U.S. News, AP via PBS NewsHour, CNBC, ABC7 New York

Britain Puts £100 Million Into an Independent AI Biosecurity Lab to Spot Biological Threats Earlier and Speed Up Vaccines

The UK government announced an AI Biosecurity Lab with £100 million (about $134 million) of public money, Bloomberg Law reported on October 23. It will be an independent non-profit modeled on the UK's AI Security Institute. OpenAI and Anthropic are each contributing up to £100 million in kind (model credits, compute, trusted model access and technical support), which is how coverage reaches "up to £300 million"; Google DeepMind and Basecamp Research join as research partners. The lab's first two jobs are earlier detection of emerging biological threats and faster development of vaccines and treatments, working within a National AI Biosecurity Initiative alongside the UK Health Security Agency, the Animal and Plant Health Agency, the Defence Science and Technology Laboratory and the medicines regulator MHRA. Why it matters: This is a public-health institution built around the same technology the government says "could lower barriers to harmful activity," with the model makers paying in credits rather than cash. No delivery timeline, location, staffing or leadership has been named, the scientific advisory committee is promised "in due course," and the announcement does not say whether the lab's outbreak detection will reach health agencies beyond the UK.

Sources: Bloomberg Law, Techi, Business Standard
