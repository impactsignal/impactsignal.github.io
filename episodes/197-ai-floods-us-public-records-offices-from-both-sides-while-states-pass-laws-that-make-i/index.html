Top Stories

AI Is Flooding America's Public Records Offices From Two Directions: Startups That Have Raised More Than $100 Million File Thousands of Requests a Day to Sell the Results, Pennsylvania's Appeals Have Doubled Since 2021, and States Are Answering With Laws That Make It Easier to Say No

MIT Technology Review reported on Wednesday, November 25, that AI is straining US open records systems from two sides at once. The first is commercial. Venture-backed startups have raised upwards of $100 million over the last few years to collect records from thousands of agencies and turn them into AI-generated sales "signals" for companies that sell to government. Starbridge has raised $52 million and Pursuit $25.5 million. PinPoint Analytics, which analyzes construction bids, told the magazine it sent as many as 2,000 requests a day while building its dataset and now sends closer to 500. NationGraph claims about 10,000 a day and did not respond to the magazine's requests for comment. The requests themselves are mostly written from templates; the AI is used on what comes back. In Panton, Vermont, population 660, the town clerk who handled every request said blanket purchasing requests went from yearly to quarterly, then narrower ones arrived monthly or more. The second side is individuals using chatbots to draft requests. David Cuillier of the Brechner Freedom of Information Project at the University of Florida ran the US Department of Education's July request log through an AI detector and concluded that at least 29 percent of requests were written at least partly by AI, up from almost none before 2025. Those requests were twice as long, and 83 percent asked for long lists of records. Federal agencies received a record 1.7 million requests last year. Pennsylvania's Office of Open Records, which hears appeals of denied requests, projects about 6,000 appeals this year, against about 3,000 in 2021 and just under 4,000 in 2025. Its director, Liz Wagenseller, said chatbots "always give [people] encouragement to try" an appeal, and that some filings contain invented legal citations. Ann Arbor, Michigan, went from 581 requests in 2021 to 937 last year. Chautauqua County, New York, hired a paralegal this summer after requests doubled. Legislatures are responding by narrowing access. The software vendor CivicPlus counts more than 20 state and federal measures in play this year. California's law, signed in September, declares that AI systems are not "members of the public," while still allowing people to use AI "at an ordinary human volume and frequency." An Indiana law that took effect July 1 lets agencies charge out-of-state requesters, prioritize residents, and deny requests they suspect are aimed at data scraping. A Pennsylvania bill that would let agencies deny requests they "reasonably suspect" came from a script or AI has stalled. Agencies are automating too: one-fifth of federal agencies report using AI or machine learning to search for records. The 29 percent figure rests on an AI detector applied to one agency's log for one month, and the request volumes for NationGraph, SmartProcure and Pursuit are the companies' own claims. Why it matters: Public records law is how residents, reporters and community groups find out what a government did, and it was written on the assumption that a person is on the other end of each request. The figures here show that assumption failing in both directions at once, and the legislative response so far (more than 20 measures, several giving agencies new grounds to refuse) changes the terms for everyone who files, including the people the laws were written for. Wagenseller put the choice plainly: fund the offices to keep up, or make the system less free, fast and open. The Pennsylvania bill is still pending, and Indiana's law has been in force for less than five months, so the first evidence of who actually gets refused is still to come.

Sources: MIT Technology Review

England Gets Its First National Guidance on AI in Children's and Adult Social Care, Published November 20, and Its Firmest Line Is That AI Should Not Make Decisions That Directly Affect People; It Is Non-Statutory, and the Time Savings It Cites Come From a Single Council's Case Study

The UK Department of Health and Social Care and the Department for Education jointly published guidance on Friday, November 20, on the use of AI in children's and adult social care in England, The Care Home Environment reported that day. The departments describe it as "a single national reference point" for leaders, practitioners and sector organizations. It sets four principles, as reported by HTN on November 25: AI-generated content is a first draft to be reviewed; AI should not make decisions that directly affect people; AI should support better experiences and outcomes for individuals and families; and personal information should not be entered into publicly available AI tools. Ministers said professional judgment and accountability "must remain with practitioners" because the technology "cannot properly understand context, nuance or inference." The uses the guidance names are administrative: speech-to-text transcription of visits, meetings and supervision notes, drafting case notes and reports, formatting records to agreed templates, summarizing long case files before a visit, and translation for families who do not speak English. Its evidence is a case study from Kingston Council, where social workers using an AI tool for case notes, assessments and supervision records in adult social care reported an average 50 to 60 percent reduction in time spent on those tasks. The government also notes that four in five care providers now use digital social care records, covering almost 90 percent of people who draw on care. A week on, the fine print is the news. The guidance is non-statutory: it creates no legal duty and no enforcement mechanism, and it does not say who checks whether a council or care provider follows it. The Kingston figure is self-reported by the social workers using the tool and was not independently measured. And the guidance goes beyond paperwork: HTN reports that it also suggests AI could analyze large datasets to highlight emerging risks and identify patterns across complex case records "to support informed decision making," which sits close to the line the second principle draws. Why it matters: Social care records decide whether a child is taken into care or an older person gets support at home, and until November 20 there was no national statement in England on where AI may and may not sit in that process. The guidance now puts a line on paper (drafting yes, decisions no), but as a recommendation, with nothing in it that makes the line binding or checked. It lands the same week as two other stories in this episode where the rule for AI-drafted records is "a human reviews it," which is also what this guidance relies on.

Sources: The Care Home Environment, HTN (Health Tech News), Digital Health

Connecticut Has One Statewide Rule on Police Use of AI, a Prosecutors' Pause on AI-Written Criminal Reports, and No Inventory of What Local Departments Run: New Haven Tested a Report-Drafting Tool With No AI Policy, and the State Police Plan to Evaluate the Same Vendor's Tools

Connecticut has no common rules, no state-level tracking and no guardrails for how police departments use AI, the Journal Inquirer reported in a piece by Jordan Nathaniel Fenster distributed by Tribune News Service on Wednesday, November 25. The single statewide measure is a moratorium that Chief State's Attorney Patrick Griffin imposed in April on AI programs that draft, author or narrate criminal reports. Griffin's stated worry was misattribution: "Someone could end up in prison because the system thought one person said something that they didn't." Automated license plate readers have their own state rules and public reporting. For everything else, there is no statewide inventory of which AI systems local police use, how, or with what safeguards. The state's Responsible AI Framework dates from 2023 and applies to state agencies and their contractors; local governments are only "encouraged to adopt" it. The state's public AI inventory likewise covers state agencies and leaves out municipal police. The reporting shows what that gap looks like department by department. New Haven signed a 2024 contract with Axon that included Draft One, which generates a draft report from body-camera audio. A department spokesman confirmed a trial, said it was not implemented department-wide, and said the department "does not currently have a policy on AI use." A member of the city's Police Commission said the trial covered minor motor vehicle matters and no criminal investigations. Bloomfield's deputy chief said the moratorium is the department's only AI policy. Norwalk follows a general city policy. Greenwich has its own: officers must write their own reports, warrants and narratives, and public chatbots are barred from drafting them, though the policy does not restrict AI in research or investigative work. The Connecticut State Police technology plan for fiscal 2027 lists evaluating Axon AI tools for "report drafting, translation, and other operational tools"; the article is careful to say that is a plan and that nothing has been deployed. A legislative fix was tried and dropped. In January the Office of the Chief Public Defender proposed a bill requiring AI-generated reports to name the software used, carry an officer's signature and be kept with audit logs. No AI report-writing proposal advanced in the 2026 session. The Judiciary Committee's co-chair, Rep. Steve Stafstrom, said prosecutors opposed the bill and the moratorium was the compromise. Vahid Behzadan, who researches AI safety at the University of New Haven, named the risk a human review does not catch: a model "might end up excluding relevant facts, including potentially exculpatory information," and a reviewing officer cannot see what the software left out. Sen. James Maroney said he plans to revisit the 2023 government AI law. The article reports no documented case of an AI-written report causing harm in Connecticut. Why it matters: A police report is the first document in a criminal case, and in Connecticut the only statewide control on AI writing one is a directive from prosecutors that can be lifted the way it was imposed, without a vote. The January bill would have made the software, the signature and the audit trail a matter of record; without it, whether a given report was machine-drafted is knowable only department by department, and most departments asked have no policy. The next dated marker is the state police's fiscal 2027 plan to evaluate the same vendor's report-drafting tools while the moratorium is still in place.

Sources: Journal Inquirer (via Tribune News Service, as carried by Governing), GovTech, Police1

England's North West Ambulance Service Extends Its AI Note-Taking Trial to the End of May 2027: Nearly a Quarter of Road Crews Signed Up in the First Weeks, Every Note Must Be Checked by the Clinician, and Patients Can Say No

North West Ambulance Service (NWAS), the NHS trust that runs emergency ambulances across north-west England, is extending its trial of ambient voice technology, an AI tool that listens to the conversation between a clinician and a patient and turns it into a structured clinical note. The Chester Standard reported on Tuesday, November 24, that the extended trial will run until the end of May 2027, after which NWAS will decide whether to roll it out fully. Emergency Services Times reported the expansion on November 27. The extension follows an eight-week proof of concept earlier this year with 40 frontline clinicians, which the trust says found the technology clinically safe. Within the first few weeks of the wider trial, nearly 25 percent of on-the-road staff had signed up. The safeguards are described in specifics. Notes are reviewed and verified by the clinician before they are added to the patient record: "Before any notes are submitted to the patient record, the clinician must check them for accuracy and completeness," said Jenny Wharton, the trust's chief information officer. Clinicians must tell patients when the tool is in use and explain that they can ask for it not to be used. Audio goes to a UK-based server for live transcription and is deleted immediately after processing. The tool runs on an existing large language model, with prompts written by the trust's own development team so the output follows one consistent template. The benefits reported so far are staff accounts, and none has been measured. Clinicians report less fatigue from documentation, and one duty officer with dyslexia said it makes taking patient histories "significantly easier." The trust has published no figures on time saved, error rates in the drafted notes, or how many patients have declined, and the "clinically safe" finding is the trust's own. NWAS says it expects the tool to matter most over winter, its busiest period. Why it matters: Ambulance records are written at the roadside or in the back of a vehicle, and they travel with the patient into the emergency department, so a wrong note has a short path to a clinical decision. This trial is notable for what the trust has committed to in public: a named end date, a decision point after it, a mandatory clinician check, audio deletion, and a patient's right to refuse. What it has not yet published is any measurement, and May 2027 is when the evidence for or against a full rollout is due.

Sources: Chester Standard, Emergency Services Times
