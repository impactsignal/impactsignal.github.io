In Rwanda, 24 Percent of Men and 6.5 Percent of Women Farmers Own the Smartphone an AI Banana-Disease App Needs, and a 357-Farmer Study Traces the Gap to Access

A study published on Monday, November 9, in Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems examined who gets to use Tumaini, an AI app that identifies banana diseases and pests from a phone photo and gives management advice. Researchers led by Jean Paul Ndayizeye of the University of Rwanda surveyed 357 smallholder banana farmers in three districts (Gasabo, Karongi and Rusizi), ran 12 focus groups, interviewed 61 farmers trained on the app, and monitored 40 smartphone owners on WhatsApp for 12 months. The stakes come from banana bunchy top disease, which the paper says can cause yield losses of up to 100 percent and has no cure once a plant is infected, leaving early detection and removal as the only control. Devdiscourse, reporting on the paper on November 12, gives the figures: smartphone ownership was about 24 percent among men and 6.5 percent among women, internet access was 20.3 percent against 4.7 percent, and after accounting for education, income and electricity, men had roughly 4.5 times the odds of owning a smartphone. Among people who did own one, the researchers found no significant gender difference in digital literacy. During the study Tumaini offered English, French and Kiswahili and did not offer Kinyarwanda, the language the participants share, and farmers described memorizing symbols to get around the interface. Why it matters: The study gives a measured answer to a question usually argued by assumption. In this sample, the difference between men and women in using an AI farm advisory tool tracks who owns the device, who holds power in the household and whether the app speaks the local language; skill plays no measurable part. Married women scored about 47 percent on a household decision-making measure against 65 percent for married men, the median smartphone cost 90,000 Rwandan francs, and government distribution accounted for more than half of the devices. About 95 percent of surveyed farmers did not know a smartphone could help with crop disease, yet 85.4 percent said they would adopt such a tool once it was explained. The paper notes its own limits, including few women smartphone owners in the sample and self-reported skills. The authors name local-language and voice-guided interfaces and group-based support as their next steps, and the paper sits in a journal collection on ICT-enabled agricultural extension.

Sources: Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems, Devdiscourse, Bioengineer.org

Small Open AI Models Can Rewrite Spanish Medical Reports in Plain Language, but All Four Model Families Tested Dropped or Invented Clinical Details

Researchers at the Universidad Carlos III de Madrid (UC3M) tested whether small AI language models can turn a medical report into plain language a patient can follow, Medical Xpress reported on Tuesday, November 10. The study, published in Scientific Reports, started from a figure cited by co-author Paloma Martínez, a professor of computer science at UC3M: 46 percent of European citizens have difficulty understanding medical documents. The team took 50 medical reports based on clinical cases supplied by the Andalusian Society of Family and Community Medicine, and cognitive accessibility experts rewrote each one three times following the international plain language standard ISO 24495-1:2023, producing 150 reference texts. Four model families (Llama 3.2, Salamandra, RigoChat-v2 and Medical-mT5, ranging from 1 billion to 7 billion parameters) were compared on simplification, meaning preserved, factual consistency, readability and environmental footprint. No model won on everything: Llama 3.2 at 3 billion parameters had the most balanced profile, and RigoChat-v2 did best on output quality and readability. Why it matters: Across all four families the researchers found three kinds of error: oversimplification that removed clinical details a patient needs, hallucinations that invented or altered data from the original report, and incoherent text. "It is precisely because of these errors that human oversight remains essential for these models, particularly in a context as sensitive as health care," Martínez said. The study therefore supports these models as a drafting step with a person checking the result. The dataset, fine-tuned models and prompts are public on GitHub and Zenodo, so the same test can be repeated on other languages and document types. The coverage derives from the university's own release, and the peer-reviewed paper is the independent record. Spain's Royal Decree 707/2026, the country's first regulation of basic cognitive accessibility, takes effect on January 2, 2027 and covers public administrations in health, justice, employment, digital services and transport.

Sources: Scientific Reports, Medical Xpress, EurekAlert (UC3M release), Bioengineer.org

A $2 Million National Science Foundation Award Funds EVAC-AI, a Los Angeles Wildfire Evacuation Project That Reserves Driverless Cars for Households With No Vehicle

The University of Southern California on Wednesday, November 11, described EVAC-AI, a research project to build an AI system that coordinates wildfire evacuations in Los Angeles. The premise comes from the January 2025 fires, when, in USC's words, delayed warnings, alerts sent to the wrong neighborhood and traffic-clogged routes made evacuation "a crisis of its own." The project is led by Ruolin Li, an assistant professor of civil engineering, with Yue Zhao, Ketan Savla and Costas Synolakis. The National Science Foundation's public award record confirms the funding independently: award 2623936, $2,000,000 obligated in fiscal year 2026, running from September 1, 2026 to August 31, 2030. The design has four connected parts: a road-access picture built from probabilistic fire forecasts and envisioned to update every five minutes at the level of a census tract; routing for both human-driven and autonomous vehicles, with centrally dispatched driverless fleets treated as a rescue resource required to cover households without a car; targeted, staggered evacuation messages in place of one mass alert; and an AI agent that advises emergency managers while giving residents a chat-style tool to report that they have no vehicle, an elderly relative or pets. Why it matters: A federally funded project now treats evacuation as one system of fire, roads, messages and people, and writes the needs of people without cars, older residents and people with disabilities into the routing problem itself, in the city whose 2025 fires showed the cost of handling those separately. What exists today is the plan and the money. Nothing has been deployed, the announcement names no fire agency or city department as a partner, and the NSF abstract describes a computational framework and research training. USC's article also carries a note that it may include AI-assisted content. The award runs to August 31, 2030.

Sources: USC Viterbi School of Engineering, US National Science Foundation

A UK Sensor Startup Maps 47,640 Acres of Top-Risk Wildfire Land Bordering Towns and Villages, Led by the South Wales Valleys, as England Sets Up a Wildfires AI Unit

More than 1.2 million acres of UK land falls in the highest category for wildfire potential, according to an analysis by EmberEye, a Cambridge startup that makes wildfire detection sensors, reported by the Cambridge Independent on Wednesday, November 11. The analysis scores how readily vegetation and terrain will burn and, using road density, how easily people can reach the land, since most UK wildfires are started by people. It then picks out ten areas where top-risk land directly borders towns and villages: the South Wales Valleys lead with 20,930 acres, followed by the South Pennines with 13,140 acres and Gwynedd in north-west Wales with 3,750 acres, and the ten together cover 47,640 acres. The newspaper sets that against this year's record: fire and rescue services in England and Wales have responded to more than 1,300 wildfires in 2026, above the 2025 record, and 19 homes were destroyed by wildfire in Stourbridge this summer. Why it matters: The ranking shows where the UK's highest-risk wildfire land meets housing, in a year when England and Wales have already passed their wildfire record. The Cambridge Independent also reports that the government is drawing up England's first Wildfire Action Plan and setting up a Wildfires AI Unit inside the National Situation Centre to detect fires and track their spread, both announced at the Prime Minister's wildfire summit on October 23. This is a vendor's analysis reported by a local newspaper, with no independent evaluation behind it. EmberEye, founded in 2024, says its ground-level infrared sensors are 60 times faster and ten times cheaper than other detection methods and that in a field trial with the Marin County Fire Department in California they detected a fire in under a minute from more than 100 meters away; those claims are the company's own. Surrey Wildlife Trust has run the sensors on the Elstead group of commons since May with Surrey Fire and Rescue Service, and no results from that trial have been published.

Sources: Cambridge Independent
