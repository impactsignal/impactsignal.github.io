New Mexico's Forester Asks Lawmakers for $1 Million to Extend AI Smoke-Spotting Cameras Toward the State's 100 Most Fire-Prone Communities

New Mexico's Energy, Minerals and Natural Resources Department told the state's Legislative Finance Committee on Thursday, November 19, that it wants to expand a network of AI-enabled wildfire detection cameras to cover the state's 100 most wildfire-prone communities, Source New Mexico reported on Friday, November 20. The department is asking for an additional $1 million in its general fund request for the coming fiscal year. The cameras turn through 360 degrees from mountaintops and cell towers, and software flags smoke and new fire starts for people to check. The system today is small: a pilot with one vendor has put about 20 cameras in and around Bernalillo County, the Albuquerque area, and counting cameras owned by large electric utilities and by towns such as Raton, the department says 59 are stationed across the state, including in the southeast and in the Gila National Forest. The department has identified 684 communities statewide as wildfire-prone. Each camera is estimated to cost $37,000 a year for hardware, installation and subscription. At that price, $1 million works out to about 27 cameras for one year; that figure is arithmetic on the department's numbers, and the report does not say how many cameras the 100 communities would need. For scale, Hoodline on November 18 described California's network as more than 1,300 cameras, with human dispatchers verifying the AI alerts. Why it matters: A state has put a unit price and a coverage target for AI wildfire detection on the record in a public budget hearing, and the forester's own words set the limits of the evidence. State Forester Laura McCarthy told lawmakers the benefit analysis is still under way and gave an early reading: "We are definitely seeing a reduction in the response time for firefighters to get on scene, and they are coming with the right resources because they have more situational awareness." No figures were attached to that claim. The purchasing detail is the other finding. "We have vendors coming to us every week, and honestly, it's a bit overwhelming to determine what exactly is our best need," McCarthy said, adding that the camera arrangement runs one year at a time, so the state is not obligated beyond that year if better technology appears. Every figure is the department's own, carried by one outlet's report of the hearing. The request is part of a total ask of $56 million, 13.7 percent more than the previous year, and it goes before the 60-day legislative session that opens January 19, 2027, where it is funded or not.

Sources: Source New Mexico, Hoodline

Software Flaw Reports Quadruple in a Year as AI Joins the Hunt, and the Center for Internet Security Tells US State and Local Governments to Plan for Downtime as Routine

Experts at the Center for Internet Security (CIS), the nonprofit that runs the cyber threat-sharing hub for US state, local, tribal and territorial governments, said on Wednesday, November 18, that those governments should treat outages as a "new normal," StateScoop reported the next day. The briefing followed a week of disruptions at Microsoft, Workday, JPMorgan Chase, Apple, United Airlines and the Federal Aviation Administration, including more than 10,000 canceled flights. StateScoop reports that the outages were attributed to IT administration issues and that no cyberattack was involved. CIS's point concerns the maintenance load behind them: AI tools are finding software flaws much faster, and every disclosed flaw becomes a patch that somebody has to test and install. Randy Rose, CIS's vice president of security operations and intelligence, said vulnerability disclosures rose from about 4,274 in October 2025 to 17,060 in October 2026. He said about 90 percent of disclosed vulnerabilities are not practically exploitable, and that software did not suddenly get worse: more people and more capable AI tools are looking at once. Most of the flaws sit in commercial and open-source products that governments buy. The effects still reached public offices. Rose said the Microsoft outage cost many government organizations their email, and in some cases file sharing and Teams, and CIS saw people trying to exploit the confusion with social engineering and look-alike web domains that deliver malware. Why it matters: There is now a number for how fast AI-assisted flaw hunting has grown, roughly fourfold in twelve months, and a named national body telling the governments that run benefits, courts and 911 centers that downtime from patching is a planning assumption. The reporting is careful about cause: the outages were attributed to administration and maintenance problems, and CIS says roughly nine in ten disclosed flaws cannot practically be exploited. The change is in volume and pace, and it lands on small public IT teams as more patches from vendors they do not control. CIS vice president Phyllis Lee described what the group asked governments to work through: which resident-facing services each outside vendor supports, whether those vendors are being watched for outages and patches, and how staff and residents would be notified if a service failed. Newsweek reported on November 19 that New York City Emergency Management and the San Francisco Fire Department posted preparedness reminders against the same backdrop, described the guidance as precautionary, and noted that officials said the incidents were separate and not linked to a coordinated attack.

Sources: StateScoop, Newsweek

Common Space Picks Satellogic to Build the First Satellite Meant for Humanitarian Users, With Launch Set for July 2027 and Five More Satellites Still to Be Funded

Common Space, a nonprofit planning a constellation of Earth-observing satellites for humanitarian and public-interest use, announced on Thursday, November 19, that Satellogic, an Argentine-US geospatial company, will build and operate its first spacecraft. Space.com and SpaceNews both reported the selection that day. The satellite is scheduled to launch in July 2027 on a rocket that has not been announced, and operations are expected to last at least five years in low Earth orbit. The spacecraft is Satellogic's NewSat Mark V, chosen after a competitive request for proposals: 82 centimeters long, 38.5 kilograms, resolving features as small as 70 centimeters from orbit, which Satellogic says its processing can sharpen to 50 centimeters. Satellogic has flown 61 Earth-observing satellites to date, according to Space.com. Common Space's case, as Space.com summarizes it, is that more than 1,000 Earth-observing satellites are already in orbit but much of what they collect is proprietary or too expensive to serve the common good. It says its constellation will collect imagery for first responders, journalists, scientists and human-rights investigators under what it calls a "club-good model," in which a large community shares the infrastructure and the cost in exchange for extensive, low-cost access. "With Satellogic as our spacecraft partner, we're moving from vision to orbit and putting high-resolution data into the hands of those who need it most," co-founder Rhiannan Price said in a statement. Why it matters: High-resolution imagery is what damage assessments, flood maps and displacement counts are built from, and the organizations that need it in a crisis have depended on commercial operators' pricing and goodwill. A nonprofit set up to own that capacity now has a named manufacturer, a specific spacecraft and a launch month. The limits are equally specific: one satellite out of a planned six, a launch eight months out on a rocket not yet named, and no published cost, membership terms or policy on who decides which areas are priorities. One satellite at this resolution does not give daily coverage; Common Space says six by 2030 would allow daily high-resolution coverage of "priority areas," and it is still raising money for the other five. Neither report describes the system as AI; the only processing claim is Satellogic's resolution enhancement. The July 2027 launch and the fundraising for the remaining five satellites are the next dated tests.

Sources: Space.com, SpaceNews

South Korea Sounds Its National Disaster Alert Three Times During the College Entrance Exam to Expose Hidden Phones, and the Ministry Counts 111 Candidates With Banned Devices

This is a new development in a story this show reported on November 15, when the top scorer in South Korea's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with a hidden camera and AI and the presidential office ordered ministries to prepare for the college entrance exam. That exam, the Suneung, took place on Thursday, November 19. South Korea's education ministry said on Friday that 111 candidates were found with unauthorized smart devices, and five of them were caught using a device during the exam, the BBC reported. About half a million students sat the test. Authorities sent three nationwide emergency alerts between 08:00 and 15:00 on exam day so that any phone hidden in an exam hall would sound. According to the BBC, phones across the country blared together even when set to silent, including phones belonging to tourists on data roaming; phones that were switched off or in airplane mode stayed quiet. It was the first time South Korea used its national disaster alert system to catch exam cheats. Popular Science, reporting on November 18 before the exam, described the alert as the one normally used to warn residents about earthquakes and other disasters. Candidates also had to cover the buttons on shirts and jackets against hidden cameras, and proctors inspected eyewear. The BBC notes that two candidates were caught with AI glasses during university entrance exams last month. Why it matters: There is now a count for AI-era cheating controls at national scale: 111 candidates with banned devices and five caught in the act among roughly half a million. And a government has used a disaster warning channel, the one built to override silent mode for earthquakes, for a purpose unrelated to safety, three times in one day, on every phone in the country. The reporting does not say how many of the 111 were detected by the alerts as opposed to inspections, so the alert's own effect is not established, and it does not address what repeated non-emergency use does to public response to real warnings. The BBC also reports that the measures unsettled honest candidates, including a student who worried his ordinary spectacles would be taken for smart glasses. On Friday, President Lee Jae Myung called for an overhaul of the exam system, saying that using AI to cheat in public examinations "rocks the basic order of our society by undermining fairness and trust." That overhaul is the next step on the record.

Sources: BBC News, Popular Science
