Kenya's High Court Gives the Government Six Months to Finish an AI Law, but Declines to Halt Public-Sector AI Systems

Kenya's High Court, sitting in Kerugoya, gave the government and Parliament six months to finish a national policy and legal framework for artificial intelligence, in a ruling Justice Edward Muriithi delivered on Thursday, November 12, 2026. The court refused the petitioners' request to order legislation within 90 days, citing the separation of powers, and refused to stop public bodies from using AI in the meantime. Petitioners John Wangai, Peter Agoro and Antony Manyara had named a Social Health Authority algorithm that sets household health insurance contributions, alleged automated marking at the Kenya National Examinations Council, alleged AI shortlisting at the Public Service Commission, smart cameras tied to automatic traffic fines, facial recognition, and poverty-scoring models for bursaries and student loans. The judge found no proof that any of these had violated a right: the court found no evidence the examinations council had deployed AI marking, and the Public Service Commission said its shortlisting is manual. The Social Health Authority told the court that 202,282 members have appealed their assessments, and that successful appeals cut total assessed contributions from 58.5 million shillings to 54.6 million shillings. Why it matters: A court has now put a date on a national AI law, with a judge who wrote that "there is no doubt that Artificial Intelligence presents serious constitutional questions of possible rights violation" keeping the case open. The ruling also shows where the bar sits for challenging public-sector AI: naming agencies was not enough without a specific system and a specific harm, and the one hard figure in the case, more than 200,000 appeals against an automated health insurance assessment, came from the agency defending its system. The case returns in mid-May 2027; Africa AI News gives the date as May 13 and the Daily Nation as May 15.

Sources: Daily Nation, Africa AI News

South Korea's Top Tax Accountant Exam Scorer Admits He Cheated With a Hidden Camera and AI, and Four Proctors Saw Nothing

Han Won-seok, 34, the top scorer in South Korea's 63rd national tax accountant qualification exam, admitted on Sunday, November 15, 2026, that he cheated using AI, after weeks of denying it. In a video on his lawyer's YouTube channel he said he photographed the questions with a miniature camera, sent the images to an AI tool and checked the answers on a smartphone; he also admitted using the same method in the first round of the certified public accountant exam in March. He had answered 159 of 160 first-stage questions correctly, including a perfect score in introductory tax law, a subject where the 16,856 test takers averaged 30.18 points and 74.21 percent fell below the 40-point minimum. The Human Resources Development Service of Korea, which runs the exam, had said it found no concrete evidence of cheating, and the four proctors who supervised him across both stages reported nothing suspicious. The agency has since apologized "for failing to prevent cheating as the examination administrator," the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency opened a formal investigation the same day, and the presidential office held an emergency meeting where policy chief Ha Joon-kyung told ministries to deploy available tools, including thermal imaging cameras. Why it matters: This is the first confirmed case in Korea's licensing exams of a candidate using generative AI to pass undetected, and it surfaced through a confession after proctors, an administrator's review and an initial investigation had all cleared him. In-person, proctored, paper-based testing is the format many countries treat as the safe fallback against AI, and here it failed against a small camera and a phone. The college entrance exam, the CSAT, is on Thursday, November 19; the finance ministry plans to suspend his certificate first because a confession alone is not enough to revoke it; and a lawmaker's proposal for 6.57 billion won (about $4.92 million) for signal detectors and extra monitoring staff is before the National Assembly.

Sources: The Korea Times, The Korea Herald, Korea JoongAng Daily

Oakland City Workers Vote 94.1 Percent to Strike and Win a Retrain-or-Reassign Clause as US Public Unions Write AI Limits Into Contracts

The Guardian reported on Thursday, November 12, 2026, that public-sector unions across the United States are winning explicit AI protections in their contracts. In Oakland, California, city employees in the International Federation of Professional and Technical Engineers Local 21 voted on October 22 to authorize a strike, with 94.1 percent in favor, seeking guarantees that workers would not be laid off or disciplined by AI. The city and the union then reached a tentative three-year agreement requiring the city to retrain or reassign workers displaced by AI, and a strike set for November 5 is on hold. Nearly 70 percent of state and local government agencies say they have adopted or are implementing generative AI, according to an Ernst and Young survey from July and August 2026 cited by the paper. Elsewhere, SEIU Local 668, which represents more than 10,000 Pennsylvania state workers, secured a policy saying the technology will not be used to discipline workers, and the Transport Workers Union in Columbus, Ohio, won a veto in 2024 over any Central Ohio Transit Authority plan to deploy autonomous vehicles. Why it matters: The rules for how US governments use AI on their own staff are being set contract by contract, ahead of most legislation; AFL-CIO president Liz Shuler told the paper, "We're using our contract negotiations to institute guardrails that policymakers won't." The workers covered are the caseworkers, dispatchers and benefits staff who deliver public services, so these clauses also shape how AI reaches the public. The Oakland agreement is tentative and still has to be finalized, and Pennsylvania's union expects the issue to return when it bargains a new contract in 2027.

Sources: The Guardian

Black and Latino New Yorkers Build Their Own On-Ramps Into AI Work, With No Outcome Data Published Yet

Gothamist, the news site of New York Public Radio, reported on Sunday, November 15, 2026, on efforts in New York City to bring Black and Latino residents into AI work and AI literacy: last week's Latino AI Summit at Civic Hall and Berkeley College in Midtown Manhattan, The Knowledge House in the Bronx, which trains underserved young people in coding and design, and the Rev. Al Sharpton's AI Fluency Initiative for adults with no tech background. A 2025 Jobs for the Future report found 67 percent of Black survey respondents feel AI is already affecting their jobs, compared with 57 percent of white respondents, and more than half of Black respondents use AI tools daily or weekly, compared with about 40 percent of all respondents. The Kapor Foundation reports that Latinos are about 19 percent of the US workforce but hold 6 percent of technical roles and 5 percent of executive roles at the country's largest tech companies. Kara Emery of the AI Hub at NYU's McSilver Institute for Poverty Policy and Research cautioned that forecasts of AI's effect on work are still projections, and that the story of AI's inevitability "is something that's often pushed by those who are currently in the big companies building them now." Why it matters: The survey figures point two ways at once: Black workers in the sample report more exposure to AI at work and more frequent use of the tools. The gap these programs address is who builds and who is hired, where Latino representation in technical roles is about a third of the Latino share of the workforce. The article gives no enrollment or job-placement figures for any of the three programs, so whether community-run training changes that gap remains unmeasured.

Sources: Gothamist / WNYC
