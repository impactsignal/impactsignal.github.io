Top Stories

Democracy Works, a 46-Person Brooklyn Nonprofit, Supplies the Voting Answers for ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, and Is Unsure It Can Fund Itself Through 2028

NBC News reported on November 2, 2026 that Democracy Works, founded in 2010 to make voting easier through technology, has become the primary source of voting logistics for the largest AI chatbots in the midterms. Anthropic, Google and OpenAI have each said they will route the nonprofit's information on how, where and when to vote into their products' answers; Democracy Works' own announcements confirm the OpenAI and Anthropic partnerships. The group told NBC it is struggling with funding. Executive director Luis Lozada: "If you give a voter a wrong piece of information and they rely on it and they're not able to vote, they probably don't try again." The dependency sits on a documented accuracy gap: a Washington Post review cited by Futurism found chatbots failed to identify a voter's congressional district from an exact address 48 percent of the time, and an Institute for Strategic Dialogue study found 29 percent of English-language voting answers incomplete, inaccurate or outdated. Why it matters: Three of the world's largest AI companies route a safety-critical civic question through one small nonprofit, and that nonprofit says its own future is uncertain. Whether Democracy Works is funded past this cycle decides whether 2028's chatbot voting answers come from a curated database of official state and local sources or from whatever the models scrape. Today's midterms are the first real-world load on the arrangement; the funding question remains open.

Sources: NBC News, Democracy Works, Reuters via The Star, Futurism

Developing (October 27 and 30): 800 Pages of CMS Records Show Four of Six Vendors in Medicare's AI Pre-Approval Pilot Drew Compliance Actions, as Senator Gillibrand Renews Call to End It

On October 27, 2026, the Electronic Frontier Foundation published more than 800 pages of CMS records from its FOIA lawsuit over WISeR, the pilot that since January 1 has required AI-evaluated prior authorization from private contractors for a list of traditional Medicare services in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington. CMS sent information requests or corrective action demands to four of the six vendors in the first months: Genzeon over a backlog and duplicate tracking numbers, Zyter over late determination letters, Virtix Health over missing the 72-hour turnaround, and Innovaccer over a provider portal it had warned CMS would not be ready. CMS says a licensed clinician makes every non-payment recommendation. On October 30, Senator Kirsten Gillibrand, ranking member of the Senate Aging Committee, called for the program to end, citing a JAMA study published October 26 (University of Minnesota and Brown, Epic Cosmos records covering more than 310 million patients) that found use of targeted services in the pilot states fell 15.0 percent for epidural steroid injections and 19.5 percent for other targeted services, relative to the other 44 states. The effect faded from 20.7 percent in the first quarter to 9.3 percent in the second for injections, and the authors say they cannot separate unnecessary care from care patients needed. Why it matters: For the first time, internal records and an independent utilization study describe the same AI-in-benefits program, and both point the same way: a rushed rollout with documented vendor failures, and a measurable fall in care nobody can yet classify as waste or harm. Vendors are paid a share of "averted expenditures," which critics say rewards denials. The pilot covers roughly 6.4 million beneficiaries and runs through December 31, 2031; a Senate resolution to end it failed 46 to 50 in July, CMS has not released its own evaluation, and the renewed demand has no scheduled vote.

Sources: Electronic Frontier Foundation, DocWire News, Tech Times, White Plains CNR

Japan Opens Its National Disaster Management Agency With 352 Staff and Power to Request Budget Changes From Ministries

Japan launched its National Disaster Management Agency on November 2, 2026, at a ceremony in Tokyo's Nagatacho district. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told ministries to "break down bureaucratic silos" so the agency can serve as a "strong control tower"; its first chief, Goshi Hosono, said the aim is to make Japan "the world's most disaster-resilient nation," with a possible Nankai Trough megaquake in mind. Staffing of 352 is 60 percent more than the Cabinet Office disaster section it replaces, and its remit runs from initial response through reconstruction. Its first act was an emergency request: households to stock at least three days of food and water, ministries to review continuity plans, local governments to improve evacuation centers and plan for stranded commuters. Why it matters: The world's most earthquake-exposed wealthy country now has a standing agency covering the whole disaster cycle, with a lever over other ministries' budgets, where before it had a division inside the Cabinet Office. Per the Cabinet Office, 20 prefectures could see shaking of upper 6 or higher in a Nankai Trough quake. The agency will assess progress on its request at the end of 2026, and by summer 2027 the government is to choose a site for a regional bureau; 49 organizations had expressed interest by the end of October.

Sources: The Japan Times (Jiji), nippon.com, AP via The Manila Times, Komei Party

CodeYogi's Smartphone-Only AI Course Reaches 350,000 Rural Indian Students in Nine States; Founder Wins the Rohini Nayyar Prize

On October 31, 2026, former NITI Aayog CEO Amitabh Kant presented the fifth Rohini Nayyar Prize for rural development to Prashant Chaudhary, founder of the CodeYogi Foundation, in New Delhi. CodeYogi has reached more than 350,000 students across nine states and 120 districts. Government school computer labs proved the barrier, so the program moved to a smartphone model in Indian languages; Elets reports about 95 percent of students learn on a phone alone. As generative AI changed how software is written, the foundation replaced its pure coding course with a 30-day applied AI fluency program. "Our school students, who we don't even expect to know how to use a computer, are pros at using code and Codex and building software with AI," Chaudhary said. Why it matters: The scale is the story: 350,000 rural students taught to build software on the device they already own, with the curriculum rebuilt around AI tools rather than syntax, in places that lack labs. Chaudhary set a target of one million learners within nine months and appealed to officials and philanthropists to help fund it, which puts a public clock on the claim.

Sources: ThePrint, Elets Digital Learning
