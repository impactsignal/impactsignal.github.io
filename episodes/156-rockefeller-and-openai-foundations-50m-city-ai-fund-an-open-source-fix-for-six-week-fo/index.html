Rockefeller and OpenAI Foundations Open a $50 Million City AI Fund That Requires a City Government Partner on Every Project

The Rockefeller Foundation and the OpenAI Foundation announced "Cities Deliver: AI Solutions for Public Services" on October 14, 2026, a $50 million program managed by RF Catalytic Capital. Grants run from $500,000 to $2 million over 24 months in two tracks: Seed ($500,000 to under $1 million) for concepts ready for a first city pilot, and Scale ($1 million to $2 million) for solutions already piloted. Governments cannot apply directly, but every project needs a committed city government partner and a "meaningful frontier AI component" from any provider; per Unite.AI, OpenAI technology is neither required nor preferred. The program aims to reach up to 100 million city residents across public health, economic mobility, environmental resilience and civic engagement, and builds on city work in Cali, Cape Town and Rio de Janeiro, where Cali's mayor Alejandro Eder said the city's dengue AI tool anticipates outbreaks up to three weeks ahead. Why it matters: Unlike the unrestricted nonprofit AI grants of the past year, this money flows only where a city has agreed to adopt the tool, tying funding to a public-sector customer rather than to a pilot that ends with the grant. Half of each grant is paid up front and half after a documented mid-term milestone, so the first cohort will produce public evidence of which city AI tools worked by mid-2028. Round 1 proposals are due November 29, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. US Eastern, with selections in April 2027: rockefellerfoundation.org.

Sources: The Rockefeller Foundation, OpenAI Foundation, Unite.AI, The Cryptonomist

MIT and Microsoft's Open-Source Correction Layer, Published in Nature, Doubles AI Forecast Skill Three to Six Weeks Out

A team led by MIT climatologist Judah Cohen and Microsoft researcher Lester Mackey published a "probabilistic bias correction" method in Nature on October 14, 2026: a machine-learning layer that sits on top of an existing forecast model and corrects its systematic errors in the two-to-six-week subseasonal range, where both physics and AI models lose skill. Per Gizmodo, it doubled the subseasonal accuracy of ECMWF's AI model and improved ECMWF's numerical model on 91% of pressure, 92% of temperature and 98% of precipitation targets; flood prediction skill rose 76% to 92%, and 86% to 150% for severe floods. In ECMWF's 2025 real-time forecasting competition it beat six operational centers, ECMWF's own AI model and 34 competing teams. The figures are the authors' own tests, and the gains were reported for land forecasts in most regions, not all. Why it matters: The three-to-six-week window is when governments and aid agencies decide whether to pre-position supplies and release anticipatory funds, and it has been the weakest link in forecasting. Because this is an open-source, model-agnostic post-processing layer that needs no supercomputer, a national weather service can run it on forecasts it already receives; Kenya's meteorological office is now experimenting with it for monsoon forecasting, which is a test, not an operational system.

Sources: Gizmodo, Nature

Google Opens Its Earth AI Agents to Early Partners After a WHO Team Used One to Locate 45,500 People in Ebola's Path in DR Congo

Google Research published an update on October 14, 2026 opening an experimental Geospatial Reasoning Agent API and a "Planetary Prediction Engine" preview to early partners, by interest form. New partner results: People in Need analyzed 14 historical cyclone paths to identify high-exposure villages; GiveDirectly calculated population exposure by wind-speed zone for Tropical Cyclone Maila and benchmarked tornado damage in Mississippi; and the Milken Institute screened more than 1,500 buildings as candidate community resilience hubs. As first reported October 7, WHO's Regional Office for Africa used the agent during DR Congo's 2026 Ebola outbreak to map remote mining corridors, identifying 48 exposed settlements and more than 45,500 people at risk in minutes to guide mobile laboratories and border surveillance, while DR Congo's INRB used the prediction engine for weekly estimates of spread into uninfected zones. Why it matters: Overlaying forest loss, mining sites and population to find who is exposed is analysis that normally waits for a GIS specialist, and the WHO team did it in minutes. Google describes all of this as early partner testing, not full production, so the open question is whether the agents stay in limited preview or reach field teams without GIS staff.

Sources: Google Research, SmartWorld

Google DeepMind and ICRISAT Launch an Open Genetic Atlas for Sorghum, a Drought-Hardy Crop That Takes a Decade to Breed

Google DeepMind and Google.org announced on October 15, 2026 a partnership with the Hyderabad-headquartered International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics (ICRISAT) to build an open-access global genetic atlas, starting with sorghum. Per Business Standard, the AI-supported catalogue is meant to help breeders identify the most promising plant combinations faster than trial-and-error breeding, which can take a decade. DeepMind will fund a three-year postdoctoral fellowship at ICRISAT to extend the tools to chickpea, groundnut and pigeon pea. In the same announcement, Google widened access to its Agricultural Understanding Models for developers, and Google.org committed $15 million to the Indian nonprofit The/Nudge for AI tools that help small enterprises reach formal credit and markets. Why it matters: Sorghum, chickpea, groundnut and pigeon pea are staple crops of the semi-arid tropics that rarely get the genomic investment maize and wheat receive. An open-access atlas held by a CGIAR research center rather than a proprietary database means national breeding programs can use it without a license, and the fellowship commits to three more crops over three years.

Sources: Business Standard, The Week

Nigeria's Top Science Prize Goes to GenMRI, an AI That Cuts a 20-Minute MRI Scan to About Two Minutes

Mary-Brenda Akoda, an AI research scientist and former Microsoft engineer who founded Genscan AI, won the 2026 Nigeria Prize for Science and Innovation, organized by Nigeria LNG, for GenMRI, the News Agency of Nigeria reported on October 14, 2026. The system reconstructs diagnostic-quality MRI images from less scan data, cutting scan time by up to 90 percent: a 20-minute procedure becomes about two minutes, and an hour-long scan about six. Retrospective validation used more than 8,800 MRI images, and a blinded review by a consultant radiologist at Buckinghamshire NHS Hospital in the UK found the images diagnostically equivalent to conventional MRI in all cases assessed. The figures come from the developer and a retrospective study, not a prospective trial. Why it matters: High-quality MRI scanners cost $1.5 million to $3.5 million, so where machines are scarce, scan time is the bottleneck: a tenfold faster scan on the same scanner is, in principle, tenfold more patients per machine without new hardware. GenMRI is not yet deployed; Genscan AI is working with clinical partners in the UK, the US, Kenya and Nigeria on clinical validation, regulatory approval and pilots, targeting wider deployment in the second half of 2027.

Sources: News Agency of Nigeria
