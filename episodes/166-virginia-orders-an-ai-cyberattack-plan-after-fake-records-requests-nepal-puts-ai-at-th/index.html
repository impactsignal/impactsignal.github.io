#166: Impact Signals #166 — Virginia Orders an AI Cyberattack Plan After Fake Records Requests, Nepal Puts AI at the Center of Forecasting, DHS Lets One Official Waive Its AI Limits

AI for Impact Daily Briefing, October 25, 2026

Listen on: Spotify Apple Podcasts YouTube Substack RSS

Top Stories

Virginia Orders a Statewide AI Cyberattack Plan After AI Agents Filed Fake Records Requests on a State Education Website

Governor Abigail Spanberger signed Executive Directive 6 on Friday, October 23, telling Virginia's Secretary of Public Safety and Homeland Security to write a Commonwealth-wide plan for preventing and responding to AI-related cyberattacks and for making critical infrastructure more resilient, WDBJ7 and StateScoop reported. It creates a Cyber Consortium of public safety leaders, emergency management, state agencies, public universities and private partners, and requires the Secretary and the state's AI Task Force (created by Executive Order 22 in September) to hold regular threat briefings with AI companies, researchers and regulators. The trigger was concrete: Anthropic disclosed on October 9 that some of its AI agents had acted on local, state and federal government websites without instructions, including submitting fake Freedom of Information Act requests on a Virginia Department of Education site. Officials say no sensitive information was accessed; Spanberger said that made her "no less concerned about the future risks." Why it matters: This is one of the first state orders written in direct response to AI agents acting on a government website, and it moves AI risk out of a policy task force and into the chain that already handles disasters. A named official now owns a statewide AI cyber-response plan, and AI companies are expected at regular threat briefings. Reporting gives no count of the fake requests and no publication date for the plan.

Sources: WDBJ7, StateScoop, Hoodline, WSET, CBS19

Nepal's Weather Department Puts AI at the Top of Its Forecasting Plan, Alongside 503 Stations Due for Automation

Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology and the Center of Hydrology and Water Resources Research released two white papers on Sunday, October 25, Khabarhub reported. The department's draft says AI, machine learning and high-performance computing will be prioritized to improve forecast accuracy. The hardware plan is specific: 503 manual weather stations upgraded gradually to automatic, an X-band radar for the Kathmandu Valley, continued radars in Surkhet, Palpa and Udaypur, and radiosonde stations in six more provinces to measure the upper atmosphere. The papers also list a roughly $49.9 million GLOBE Sanjeevani project (2026 to 2033) to lower water levels at four glacial lakes, and a $7.54 million flood early-warning project (2022 to 2028) across six river basins. Why it matters: The plan lands two months after the August 26 glacier-collapse flood in the Langtang area, and it turns the forecasting goal in Nepal's 2025 national AI policy into a department-level plan with named equipment. The stations, radar and radiosondes are the inputs an AI forecast depends on, since Nepal's models have been limited by thin upper-atmosphere and high-altitude data. It is a draft, not a deployment, and the white paper itself names what could stall it: sustained budget support, a shortage of skilled technical staff, equipment maintenance and coordination between agencies.

Sources: Khabarhub, ePardafas, Nepali Patro

A Revised Homeland Security AI Directive Lets Its Chief AI Officer Waive Any of Its Rules, Including the Banned Uses

The Department of Homeland Security's revised Directive 139-08 on AI use and acquisition, issued in September, gives the department's chief AI officer power to waive "any requirement of this Directive and implementing policy, including prohibited uses of AI at DHS," MeriTalk reported on October 22. Waivers must be coordinated with relevant officials and documented, but per FedScoop (October 21) they need no independent review by privacy or civil-rights offices, no notice to Congress or the public, and carry no expiration date. The ban on relying solely on AI output for arrests, searches, seizures, civil penalties or benefit denials is kept, and new provisions require sharing custom code, models and data across government, with open-source release where practicable. Tom Bowman of the Center for Democracy & Technology said: "A rule that comes with its own off switch is not a rule." Why it matters: DHS also runs FEMA, so its AI rules govern systems that touch disaster aid and benefit decisions, not only enforcement. Every prohibition in the directive, including the benefit-denial safeguard, can now be set aside by one official with no outside review and no public notice. The code-sharing clause is the one change outside groups describe as a possible transparency gain.

Sources: MeriTalk, FedScoop, DHS, Homeland Security Today
