US Government Auditors Get Viable Attack Plans From All Four of the Most Used AI Chatbots in Under 12 Hours Each

The US Government Accountability Office (GAO), the audit arm of Congress, on Wednesday, November 18, released a report titled "Artificial Intelligence: Covert Testing Used Common Platforms to Create Viable Attack Plans." In spring 2026 its investigators submitted prompts to the four most used generative AI platforms as of April 2026: Claude, Grok, ChatGPT and Gemini. According to the report's highlights, two platforms produced information for an explosive device designed to cause mass casualties and the other two produced information for attacking the electrical grid, and each took less than 12 hours, though GAO notes the time could vary with the topic and the user's experience. The safeguards did fire: in every case GAO's prompts triggered a denial, a removal of information or an account suspension, and in every case GAO got around it "by using common safeguard-bypassing techniques," then checked that the output was usable through research, surveillance and interviews with experts. The report does not say which platform produced which plan; the four were randomly assigned to a scenario and to a letter, A through D. GAO says officials from all four companies "generally stated that their companies will continue to improve the safety of their platforms." Per the Associated Press as cited by EMS1, Anthropic said it takes the findings seriously and does not dispute that a determined tester can extract harmful content by misrepresenting a request's purpose, but said it could not verify GAO's results, and Google said the testing used earlier models; an OpenAI spokesperson told ABC News the testing "took place months ago on an older version of ChatGPT." Why it matters: The claim that the chatbots refuse has now been tested by a government auditor, where before it rested on the companies and on outside researchers, and the result is on the congressional record: four of four refused, and four of four were bypassed. The finding concerns the safeguards, the same layer every public-interest deployment of these models relies on when it promises the tool will not produce harmful output. The report states its own limits: four tests, a sample GAO describes as not statistically significant, a point in time in spring 2026, and models that at least two companies say have since been replaced. EMS1, a trade outlet for emergency medical services, noted that bombings and grid attacks are scenarios fire and ambulance agencies already train for, and that the findings do not change what crews are asked to do on scene. The tests were requested by Senator Maggie Hassan of New Hampshire, who said one of the first hearings of the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee in the new Congress will be on AI risk to homeland security and critical infrastructure.

Sources: US Government Accountability Office, EMS1, Tom's Guide (via Yahoo News)

Review of 33 Studies in BMC Infectious Diseases: AI Surveillance That Combines Sewage and Symptom Data Flags Outbreaks Earlier, With Almost No Evidence From Live Use

A systematic review published Wednesday, November 18, in the journal BMC Infectious Diseases looked at how machine learning is used to track and forecast infectious disease, with particular attention to gut infections such as rotavirus gastroenteritis, which weigh heavily on children and on low-resource health systems. The authors, Chunmin Pu and six colleagues, searched PubMed and IEEE Xplore for studies published through July 2026 and followed the PRISMA 2020 reporting standard. Thirty-three studies met the bar, 28 of them modeling studies and five contextual, according to a Scienmag summary carried on Bioengineer.org: five statistical models, eight machine learning approaches, four deep learning systems and 11 that the review calls multi-source digital surveillance frameworks. Those multi-source systems, especially the ones built on wastewater monitoring and symptom reports, consistently reported detecting outbreak signals earlier than single-source designs. Most of the evidence is retrospective, meaning models tested on past data, and in the summary's words, "prospective real-world deployment and external validation were largely absent." The paper itself sat behind a browser check and was not read directly; the figures here are as reported in the summary. Why it matters: The review separates two claims that are often made as one: that a model predicted past outbreaks well, and that it will warn a health ministry in time. Standard surveillance waits on laboratory-confirmed cases that move slowly through hospital and lab systems, so earlier detection is the gain on offer, and the evidence for it comes mainly from combining sewage and symptom data. The evidence that any of it works when switched on in a live health system is close to missing. The authors conclude that practical adoption "will depend less on retrospective benchmark accuracy" and more on external validation, on whether health staff can interpret the model, and on whether it fits the surveillance systems institutions already run. This show reported on November 7 that the Pan American Health Organization trained 95 surveillance staff on a platform with new AI features; the review speaks to what is known, and not known, about tools of that kind in use. It names prospective trials and validation on outside data as what would settle the question, and reports no such trial under way.

Sources: BMC Infectious Diseases, Bioengineer.org (carrying Scienmag)

New York's Attorney General Creates the ASSET Unit to Enforce State AI Laws, Six Weeks Before the RAISE Act Takes Effect

New York Attorney General Letitia James on Tuesday, November 17, announced a new unit inside her office called the AI Safety, Standards, Enforcement and Transparency Unit, or ASSET, StateScoop and the Albany station WRGB reported. It will focus on consumer privacy, cybersecurity, digital scams and online safety, and will be led by Cristy Phillips, previously special counsel for the office's Economic Justice Division. Its first named job is to lead enforcement of New York's Responsible AI Safety Education Act, known as the RAISE Act, which takes effect January 1, 2027, and which, according to StateScoop, requires developers of certain advanced AI models to publicly disclose their safety practices and to promptly report serious safety and security concerns. The unit will also help enforce a law requiring chatbots to make reasonable efforts to identify and respond to suicidal thoughts, restrictions on potentially harmful chatbot features for children, and laws against distributing harmful intimate images, including images made or altered with AI. "Promising new technologies like artificial intelligence should never come at the expense of New Yorkers' safety and privacy," James said in the announcement. Why it matters: One of the first US state laws aimed at the developers of the most advanced AI models now has a named enforcement team and a named leader before the law starts. Yesterday this show reported a survey in which most state AI officials said they lack budget and authority; this is a different kind of body, a prosecutor's office that can investigate companies, and it also takes on the chatbot rules that touch children and people in crisis. StateScoop set the move against a federal fight over who gets to regulate, reporting that a 2025 executive order from President Trump sought to stop states from enforcing their own AI laws and that James had joined a bipartisan group of state officials opposing federal legislation that would limit state safeguards. Neither report gave the unit's budget or the number of staff, and those decide how much a unit like this can do. Its first enforcement duty begins January 1, 2027, when the RAISE Act takes effect.

Sources: StateScoop, WRGB CBS 6 Albany (via WJLA)

A 17-Year-Old in Gurugram Builds Visionably, an AI Tool That Rebuilds PDFs for Screen Readers, and Counts 2,221 Accessibility Barriers on 30 Indian Bank Websites

Yash Bhatnagar, a 17-year-old self-taught programmer from Gurugram, near Delhi, has built a service called Visionably that converts PDFs into documents a screen reader can make sense of, India Today reported on Thursday, November 19, following a profile in The Better India on November 16. A screen reader can read text aloud, but most PDFs are made without the hidden structure it needs: tags that mark headings and tables, a reading order, descriptions of images. The Better India's example is a monthly bank statement, a document a blind person may be unable to read without asking someone else to read it to them. Visionably's customers are organizations: one sends its PDFs, the tool's AI adds the tags, the reading order and the image descriptions, and the files go back in about 48 hours depending on the job, according to Bhatnagar, with the aim of meeting two recognized standards, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines version 2.2 and PDF/UA. India Today reports a second piece of work alongside the tool: an assessment of the websites of 30 major Indian banks that identified 2,221 accessibility barriers. The idea came in October 2025, when Bhatnagar was pitching a lecture transcription tool at the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad and the team there told him that more blind people needed help with inaccessible PDFs. Why it matters: The count is new: 2,221 barriers across the websites of 30 major banks, in a country where banking has moved onto phones and PDFs. The tool puts the fix at the organization that publishes the document, so that a statement arrives readable and a blind customer does not have to work around it. "It would make sense to make a news article accessible for every reader, but the newspaper should make it accessible themselves," Bhatnagar told The Better India. The standards it targets, WCAG 2.2 and PDF/UA, are public, which means the output can be tested against them by anyone with the tools to do so. The 2,221 figure comes from India Today alone and is the builder's own audit, and neither report cites an outside evaluation, a count of organizations using Visionably or a bank that has used it. No next step with a date was reported.

Sources: India Today, The Better India
