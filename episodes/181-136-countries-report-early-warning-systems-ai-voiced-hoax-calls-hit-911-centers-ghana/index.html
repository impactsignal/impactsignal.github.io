Top Stories

UNDRR and WMO Count 136 Countries With Early Warning Systems and a 6.1-Fold Mortality Gap; Only 64 Have All Four Parts in Place

The UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) released the Global Status of Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems 2026 on Wednesday, November 11, at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Türkiye. The number of countries reporting some multi-hazard early warning capability has risen to 136, which is 17 more than in March 2025 and 70 percent of all countries; Africa added the most over the past year, 11 countries, bringing its regional coverage to 77 percent. Countries with substantial to comprehensive capability recorded disaster-related mortality 6.1 times lower than countries with limited to moderate systems, and 3.2 times fewer people affected. Anticipatory action frameworks, which trigger aid on a forecast before the hazard arrives, rose from 154 to 262 in a year across 75 countries, and 146 activations in 54 countries in 2025 reached 9.6 million people, backed by US$119.9 million in pre-arranged financing. As of June 2026, 38 countries had fully operational cell broadcast alerting and 17 more were in the final stages of building it. Reuters, which saw a draft of the report, adds that only 64 of the 136 countries have capability across all four pillars (risk knowledge, monitoring and forecasting, warning dissemination, and preparedness and response), and that the report estimates $500 million would extend coverage to every country and maintain it for three years. Why it matters: The report moves the measure of success from whether a warning was issued to whether people could act on it. Lloyd's Register Foundation World Risk Poll data cited in it found that in low-income and lower-middle-income countries fewer than half of people who received a warning were able to act, against three-quarters in upper-middle-income countries, and that 95 percent of disaster survivors in East Asia had been warned, against 47 percent in Central and Western Africa. Small island developing states have the lowest reported coverage and least developed countries the lowest comprehensiveness scores. Per Reuters, the head of UNDRR said the 2027 target of covering everyone is unlikely to be met, and WMO's secretary-general, Celeste Saulo, warned that the current El Niño "could be literally off our monitoring charts", so the 262 anticipatory action frameworks and the 38 national cell broadcast systems are what will be tested in the coming months.

Sources: UNDRR and WMO, Reuters via The Japan Times, Reuters via The Standard

An AI-Voiced Bomb Threat Closed a California High School, and 14 Percent of Surveyed 911 Professionals Say Hoax Callers Have Used AI Tools on Them

NPR reported on Wednesday, November 11, that hoax emergency calls, known as swatting, are now being made with AI-generated voices and sound effects, and that dispatchers are finding them harder to tell from real ones. Its opening case is from August: a bomb threat to Wheatland Union High School in Yuba County, California, made by an AI-generated voice, brought an overnight police response and canceled classes; there was no bomb and no one was hurt. In a survey published in June by APCO International, the association of public safety communications officials, and the Public Safety Threat Alliance, about 14 percent of 766 public safety communications professionals said they had been targeted using AI tools. APCO's chief executive, Mel Maier, told NPR that swatting voices "used to be pretty easy to recognize, but now they're natural sounding", and Brandon May, an assistant professor of forensic psychology at the Florida Institute of Technology, said he can clone his own voice with a commercial tool "for less than $20". Why it matters: Synthetic voices have moved from a predicted risk to a documented one inside emergency dispatch, with a named incident and a survey figure from mid-2026. The reporting also shows where the defense sits: April Heinze of NENA, the 9-1-1 association, told NPR that most swatters do not dial 911 directly, because a 911 call carries a location, a callback number and the name on the account, and location on 911 is very hard to spoof; hoax calls usually come in to police departments or courthouses and are transferred. Each hoax pulls responders away from real emergencies. APCO and NENA say they are developing training for members on the new threat and declined to describe its specifics.

Sources: NPR

Ghana's Youth Ministry Backs Funza AI, a Service That Runs Entirely on SMS, Built for the 9 Million Ghanaians Who Do Not Use the Internet

Ghana's Ministry of Youth Development and Empowerment has partnered with Scopeg AI, a youth-led Ghanaian technology company, to offer its Funza AI service to young people in the ministry's programs, Citi Newsroom and 3News reported on Tuesday, November 10. A user texts a question to a short code and gets an AI-generated reply within seconds. 3News reports that it runs over basic 2G signal, so it needs no smartphone, app, Wi-Fi or data bundle, and works on any handset on any network in the country. Both outlets cite the reason for the design: more than 9 million people in Ghana, roughly a quarter of the population, do not use the internet, and most of them are young. The service is aimed at tutoring for students, agricultural advice for farmers, business tools for small enterprises and career guidance for jobseekers, and participants in the ministry's youth programs get discounted access plus a limited allocation of free daily queries. Why it matters: Most AI tools assume a smartphone and a data connection; this one assumes neither. Scopeg AI's chief executive, Klenam Kofi Tamakloe, said the team modeled the service on how mobile money brought financial services to feature phones, and a government ministry has now put its programs behind that design. Funza AI is not yet live nationwide: both reports say the national launch is expected by the end of November 2026, and neither gives a user count, a price, or the language model behind the service.

Sources: Citi Newsroom, 3News

Applications to Research Funders Are Up Roughly 57 Percent Since ChatGPT Arrived, and Foundations Are Split on Answering AI With More AI

The Chronicle of Philanthropy reported on Monday, November 9, that AI is making it easier for nonprofits to find and write grant applications, and that the volume is landing on foundation program staff. A Research on Research Institute analysis from June found applications to research funders have risen roughly 57 percent since ChatGPT came online; outside research, the Chronicle says, the evidence is mostly anecdotal. Jean Westrick, chief executive of the Technology Association of Grantmakers, asked a room of about 60 grant makers at the recent Philanthropy Northwest conference whether they were seeing more applications and says every hand went up; one program officer told her they had received 30 nearly identical proposals. Amy Sample Ward, chief executive of NTEN, described a nonprofit that had applied to 22 grants by October, against about 12 the year before, and questioned whether more applications had meant more money. Why it matters: The grant process is changing on both sides at once, and funders are responding in two directions. The Ewing Marion Kauffman Foundation began using an enterprise chatbot this year, with about half to 60 percent of staff using it, and its technology chief told the Chronicle no AI tool's funding recommendation should be taken at face value; the Patrick J. McGovern Foundation built Grant Guardian, an AI tool that assesses the financial health of prospective grantees, and offers it free to other grant makers. Others are shrinking the paperwork instead: one foundation fills in applications from public data and then holds a short conversation with the applicant. The Technology Association of Grantmakers says an update to its 2023 responsible AI framework for funders is coming soon.

Sources: The Chronicle of Philanthropy

DSST Public Schools in Denver Gets $350,000 to Put an AI Lesson-Planning Tool for Multilingual Math Classes Into All 16 of Its Campuses

DSST Public Schools, a charter network with 16 campuses in Denver and Aurora, Colorado, will receive $350,000 to build and pilot an AI tool called Premo, the Denver Gazette reported on Tuesday, November 10. Premo helps K-8 teachers plan grade-level math lessons so that multilingual students can take on the content while building academic English, aligning each lesson to established English language development frameworks and generating teaching strategies matched to each student's English proficiency level. The grant comes from Multilingual by Design, a Washington, D.C. philanthropic fund that pairs education nonprofits with AI developers and that the Gazette reports is backed by the Bezos Family Foundation and Gary Community Ventures. DSST was one of three recipients chosen from applicants nationwide, and at its 16 campuses more than 65 percent of students are current or former multilingual learners. Why it matters: The design puts the AI on the teacher's planning desk, not in front of the child, and pairs it with people: teachers get coaching and technical assistance, and Throughline Learning, a Rhode Island nonprofit, will partner with DSST on implementation. The report gives no results yet; this is a funded pilot, not an evaluated one. The rollout runs over the coming year across the 16 campuses, and DSST is one of three teams in the fund's national cohort.

Sources: The Denver Gazette
