#179: Impact Signals #179 — NYU Langone's ER Story Test for PTSD, Vet-Checked Livestock AI in Uzbekistan, a UN Rulebook for City AI

Top Stories

NYU Langone's PREVIA Reads One Routine ER Answer and Flags All 30 Patients Who Developed PTSD

Researchers at NYU Langone Health built PREVIA, an AI screener that reads the short story a trauma patient tells at intake ("What brought you to the ED?") and estimates their risk of PTSD a month later. The study, published online in Nature Communications and announced November 9, ran the tool on narratives from 145 emergency-department patients; the team reports its models identified more than 95.4 percent of at-risk patients. In a head-to-head test, PREVIA flagged all 30 patients who went on to develop PTSD, while an ED clinician who specializes in PTSD caught 89 percent. The pipeline layers open models (Llama to weigh risk signals, Mistral to surface protective context such as coping skills and social support) and outputs a risk score with a plain-language explanation. It is a research result, not a tool deployed in any emergency room. Why it matters: About 43 million US patients reach an emergency department each year with physical injuries, more than a quarter develop PTSD, and only about 28 percent of EDs routinely screen, largely because a clinician has 15 to 20 minutes per patient. PREVIA's signal sits inside an answer every ED already collects, so the time barrier the researchers name does not apply. The open question is scale: 145 patients in one health system, and the next published test on a larger, more diverse group will show whether the explanations senior author Katharina Schultebraucks emphasizes hold up.

Sources: NYU Langone Health, News-Medical, PR Newswire

Uzbekistan's Bizon Livestock App Reaches 9,000 Active Farmers, With Licensed Vets Validating Its AI Advice

An Asian Development Bank case study published November 9 documents Bizon, a free mobile app for livestock farmers in Uzbekistan that combines animal records, health and production logs, vaccination and feeding reminders, and links to vets, input suppliers, buyers and participating banks. Its AI-supported advice is validated by licensed veterinarians before farmers act on it. As of October 2026 the app had more than 13,000 downloads and more than 9,000 active farmers, according to the case study. Bizon is not new (it has been on the App Store since at least February 2026, published by the Tashkent developer CONSORT GROUP), and the user figures are self-reported to ADB rather than independently audited. Why it matters: The case study describes rural veterinary care in Uzbekistan as limited and mostly reactive, treating sick animals rather than preventing illness, and Bizon's design puts a licensed vet, not the model, in the accountable seat. This is the first published account of its reach, in a country the show has not covered before. ADB's record also shows a separate $17 million grant approved in November 2025 for a national digital livestock system in neighboring Tajikistan, so whether this model spreads across Central Asia is the dated question to watch.

Sources: Asian Development Bank (Development Asia), Google Play, Apple App Store

ITU and UN-Habitat Release the UN's First City AI Toolbox, Naming "Don't Use It" as a Responsible Outcome

The International Telecommunication Union and UN-Habitat released the U4SSC Toolbox for People-Centred Smart Cities in the AI Era, reported from November 5, described as the UN's first system-wide toolbox for keeping people accountable for public decisions in cities. Built around real cases from Helsinki to eThekwini, South Africa, it walks mayors and city managers through an AI project's full life cycle: what problem the city is solving, who could benefit or be harmed, how the system is monitored, who is accountable, and when to change course or stop. Its four core rules: start from the urban problem rather than the technology, keep public decisions under meaningful human control, scale safeguards with potential impact, and recognize that deciding not to use an AI system can be responsible. UN-Habitat executive director Anacláudia Rossbach said AI must help cities serve communities "especially those living in informal settlements and other underserved areas." Why it matters: The toolbox is guidance and binds no city. What is newly true is that the UN system has a shared written standard for city AI that treats "stop" and "don't start" as legitimate outcomes and requires accountability to be named before deployment. It marks the 10th anniversary of United for Smart Sustainable Cities, coordinated by ITU, UN-Habitat and UNECE with 19 other UN agencies, and ships with companion publications on urban AI governance, security for digital public infrastructure, and city data platforms.

Sources: IT-Online, BusinessDay NG, Electronics Media

Feinstein Institutes Wins $3.4 Million NIH Award to Build AI That Catches Hospital Delirium Clinicians Miss

Drs. Alex Makhnevich, Liron Sinvani and Theodoros Zanos at Northwell Health's Feinstein Institutes for Medical Research received $3.4 million from the National Institutes of Health, announced November 6, to develop and validate AI tools that detect and prevent delirium. The institute says clinicians miss more than 75 percent of delirium cases, even in hospitals with screening programs, and coverage of the award notes up to 40 percent of admitted patients may develop the condition. The work has three aims: identify delirium in the emergency department, predict which hospitalized patients are most likely to develop it, and build both tools into clinical workflows. It is a funded research program, not a deployed product. Why it matters: Delirium is common, dangerous for older patients, and mostly invisible to busy staff, so the 75 percent miss rate defines the problem. The third aim is the telling one: workflow integration is where many clinical AI tools stall after scoring well in a paper. With today's lead, it is the second US team betting that the emergency department's first encounter is where a routinely missed condition can be caught; the measurable outcome is whether the validated tool lowers that miss rate on real wards.

Sources: Technologies.org, Citybiz, BioSpace
