#177: Impact Signals #177 — Brazil's Offline Skin-Disease App, Valencia's Flood Model, USDA's AI Crop Checker

AI for Impact Daily Briefing, November 07, 2026

Top Stories

Brazil's Health Ministry and Einstein Hospital Test an Offline App That Screens Neglected Skin Diseases, at About 80 Percent Accuracy Across 1,400 Patients

Brazil's Ministry of Health and the Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein have built CUTIS-AI, a tool that helps primary-care health workers triage skin diseases, Agência Brasil reported on November 4. It combines a photo of the lesion with clinical, epidemiological and sociodemographic data from the Brazilian population, and targets diseases that reach a rural clinic long before a dermatologist: sporotrichosis, cutaneous leishmaniasis, leprosy, chromoblastomycosis, lobomycosis, cutaneous tuberculosis and brown recluse spider bites. The study has analyzed about 1,400 patients and more than 4,000 lesion images, accuracy currently sits around 80 percent, and the app works without an internet connection. Both the ministry and Einstein describe it as a support tool that does not replace a clinician's assessment. Why it matters: These diseases mostly affect poor and rural Brazilians and are routinely misread where no specialist is nearby. The national health system is building and validating the tool on its own patients, across Brazilian skin types and regions, because, as health surveillance secretary Fabiano Geraldo Pimenta Junior put it, a model trained on one population may not perform the same on another. CUTIS-AI is still in the study phase, and 80 percent marks the distance it has to cover before routine clinical use.

Sources: Agência Brasil, IDB Invest

Valencia's AI-DANA Model Replays 36 Hours of the 2024 Poyo Ravine Flood in 57 Minutes on One GPU

AI-DANA, a research project led by Valencian firm TYRIS AI with water utility Facsa and the Universitat Jaume I, published its first validation results on November 6. The system reads Valencia's weather radar every ten minutes and uses physics-informed neural networks to forecast rainfall 90 minutes ahead, feeding a hydrodynamic model of the Ribera del Júcar split into more than 350,000 cells. Replaying the Poyo ravine flood from the October 2024 DANA on a 786 square kilometer model of about 7.9 million ten-meter cells, it scored 0.94 out of 1 against recorded flow and matched flooded areas in l'Horta Sud at depths close to those documented after the storm. The project carries a budget of 679,640.57 euros from the EU's FEDER fund and the Valencian innovation agency IVACE+i. Why it matters: The 2024 floods were a failure of time: people learned too late where the water was going. A model that replays the region's worst local flood in under an hour on a single graphics card, reports its own uncertainty, and checks itself against official maps, satellite images and riverside cameras is sized for use during a storm. Its own team says it remains in research and validation and has not been deployed as a warning system.

Sources: RETEMA (article credited to project partner Facsa)

PAHO Trains 95 Surveillance Staff From 16 Countries on EIOS, Including New AI Features for Spotting Outbreak Signals

The Pan American Health Organization said on November 3 that 95 surveillance and public health professionals from 16 countries took part in training run by its Department of Health Emergencies between October 6 and 29. The sessions covered EIOS (Epidemic Intelligence from Open Sources), the WHO-backed platform that scans news and other open information for signs of public health threats. Staff from Costa Rica, Dominica, Guatemala, Jamaica, Mexico, Nicaragua, Peru and Uruguay trained on core tools for national event-based surveillance; a second group from Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, El Salvador, Panama, Paraguay and the Dominican Republic took specialized sessions on the new AI functions. Why it matters: Outbreak detection often fails before any lab test, at the moment someone should have noticed a signal in a local news report. Putting AI signal-sorting in the hands of national surveillance officers in 16 countries, under a regional epidemic intelligence plan for 2024 to 2029 approved by member states, changes the day-to-day work of the region's early-warning system. PAHO has published who was trained; it has not yet published what the AI features caught.

Sources: PAHO, fundsforNGOs News

After a File Mix-Up Skewed Nebraska and Kansas Corn Yields, USDA Puts an In-House AI Checker on Its November 10 Crop Report

The U.S. Department of Agriculture said on November 6 that it will run an internally developed AI tool over its November 10 crop production report before publication, AgroLatam reported, citing Reuters. Under Secretary Scott Hutchins said the agency determined the system could have caught the errors in its September corn estimates, which according to Lance Honig of the National Agricultural Statistics Service came from a computer file that wrongly combined irrigated and non-irrigated acreage; the field survey data was correct. USDA corrected the numbers on October 23, raising Nebraska's corn yield estimate about 3 percent and cutting Kansas's about 2 percent. The AI will sit as an added validation layer alongside existing statistical methods. Why it matters: USDA crop reports set prices, guide crop insurance planning and shape loan decisions for farm families, and the National Corn Growers Association, representing about 35,000 farmers, criticized the errors. Falling farmer participation in USDA surveys has also pushed the agency toward new estimation methods, including a satellite and crop-model pilot begun in September. November 10 is the first public test: a clean report is evidence the checker works, and another revision would show it does not.

Sources: AgroLatam (citing Reuters), RFD-TV

The World Bank's IFC Backs Senegal's Tolbi, a Satellite-and-AI Farm Platform Aiming at 2 Million Farmers and Cooperatives by 2030

The International Finance Corporation has announced a partnership with Tolbi, a Senegalese agritech company, Devdiscourse reported from Dakar on November 7. Developed with the Business Investment Financing Track of the Global Agriculture and Food Security Program (GAFSP), the investment aims to reach 2 million farmers and cooperatives by 2030 with farming guidance, access to finance and links to buyers; the amount was not disclosed. Tolbi combines satellite imagery, AI and data from local field agents to monitor crops, estimate harvests and recommend planting and input use, and IFC will advise it on growing that field-agent network. IFC's disclosure database separately lists a 600,000 dollar advisory project with Tolbi on maize productivity, running March 2026 to September 2027. Why it matters: Senegalese lenders struggle to price farm loans because they lack reliable data on crop conditions and expected yields, and a satellite crop estimate is exactly that data. This deal puts development-bank money behind AI monitoring as an input to smallholder credit. The 2 million figure is a 2030 goal; the record to watch is how many farmers and loans the field-agent network actually reaches.

Sources: Devdiscourse, IFC project disclosures

Circle Foundation Caps Its Human Staff at Two, With AI Agents Scoring Grant Proposals and People Keeping Every Decision

The Circle Foundation, giving arm of payments company Circle Internet Group, is designed to cap its human staff at two and hand most operational work to AI agents, the Chronicle of Philanthropy reported on November 6. Created with shares set aside in Circle's 2025 IPO and giving through a donor-advised fund, it accepts no unsolicited proposals: an intake agent searches for charities to partner with, then scores proposals against rubrics the foundation wrote. Co-leader Mercina Tillemann Perez said the agents turn tens of hours of desk work into a couple of minutes, and quarterly grantee calls will be transcribed and analyzed by AI. Founding chair Elisabeth Carpenter drew the line: "AI native doesn't mean AI autonomous." Why it matters: A Technology Association of Grantmakers survey cited by the Chronicle found 81 percent of foundations experimenting with AI and only 30 percent with an AI policy. Circle was built around agents from the start, with a written rule on who decides, and TAG's Jean Westrick said she knows of no other foundation taking this approach, warning of the risks of over-streamlining while trust in institutions erodes. The foundation says it will track its progress and publish what it learns, including, in time, whether agent-scored, invitation-only intake funds a narrower set of groups.

Sources: The Chronicle of Philanthropy, Business Wire
