Watch Duty's Gemini Tool Turns Static-Filled Fire Radio Into Text and Flags a Fresh Dispatch in Under 30 Seconds, With More Than One Word in Five Still Wrong on Degraded Audio

Watch Duty is a US nonprofit wildfire alert app run by active and retired firefighters, dispatchers and first responders, whose volunteer reporters listen to public fire radio, verify what they hear and push alerts. On Friday, November 20, Google published the results of a six-month Google.org Fellowship in which its engineers and researchers worked full-time inside the nonprofit. The team used more than 20,000 hours of past radio recordings to build a tool on Google's Gemini model that strips heavy static from analog fire radio and turns firefighting jargon into searchable text. According to Google's post, the tool transcribes more than 200 hours of radio speech a day from about 400 active feeds, sends reporters a signal about a possible fresh dispatch in less than 30 seconds, and cut the word error rate on degraded VHF radio audio from 38 percent to 22.34 percent. It has been designed and tested to handle more than 10,000 feeds at once; before the tool, a reporter monitored up to 20 feeds by ear. Volunteer reporter satisfaction with radio transcription tools went from 35.3 percent to 92.3 percent. Google describes one live test: in July 2026 the Railroad Fire ignited at night in Washington state, the tool transcribed and flagged the first 911 dispatch in under 30 seconds, and Watch Duty's team verified the fire and sent a Critical Alert, the kind that breaks through a phone's do-not-disturb setting. Google says it was the first Critical Alert Watch Duty had ever sent overnight. Why it matters: An AI tool for emergency alerting has published operating numbers from real use, including its error rate. A 22 percent word error rate on bad audio is far from clean, and the design reflects that: the model listens and flags, and trained people verify every incident and decide on every alert. The reported gain is coverage, since a dispatch is no longer missed because nobody was listening to that channel at that moment. The Digital Watch Observatory, a Geneva-based digital policy monitor, reported the announcement on November 23 and added the limits: the performance figures come from Google, and the announcement does not independently establish that evacuation outcomes improved. It also notes the work builds on $2 million in Google.org funding and $300,000 in cloud credits awarded to Watch Duty in 2024. This show covered New Mexico's smoke-detection cameras on November 21; the mechanism here reads radio traffic instead of camera images and sits inside a volunteer-run nonprofit instead of a state agency. The evidence so far is the company's own account plus one fire. No independent evaluation has been published, and Google's post names no next milestone.

Sources: Google (The Keyword), Digital Watch Observatory, Watch Duty

South Korea's Education Minister Resigns Three Days After the College Entrance Exam, Saying the Ministry Was Not Prepared for AI Cheating

This is a new development in a story this show reported on November 15 and November 21. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin offered to step down on Sunday, November 22, three days after nearly 500,000 South Koreans sat the College Scholastic Ability Test, known as the Suneung. "As the minister, I cannot be free from the criticism that we were not prepared in advance," he said in a statement released by the ministry, as reported by The Korea Herald. President Lee Jae Myung accepted the resignation an hour after it was presented, the presidential office said, and instructed Prime Minister Han Seong-sook to concentrate on measures related to the exam. Time lays out the sequence. The ministry announced its anti-cheating rules on Tuesday, November 17, two days before the exam: glasses inspected, shirt buttons covered so they could not hide cameras, sleeves rolled up, public Wi-Fi shut down around test centers, and the national emergency alert system fired at random times to expose hidden phones. Extra invigilators received video training the day before. The Korean Federation of Teachers' Associations had called for Choi to resign on November 17, asking whether selecting supervisors one day, training them the next and deploying them the day after could produce the expertise to spot sophisticated AI cheating. After the exam, a group of test-takers said in a November 21 statement that schools had not applied the rules uniformly. The Korea Herald reports that 111 test-takers were found possessing or using smart devices, including smart glasses in one case and a miniature camera with wireless earphones in another. Why it matters: AI-assisted cheating has cost a cabinet minister his job, in a country where one exam decides university entry for roughly half a million people a year. The alerts and inspections did catch people: 111 of them. The resignation followed from rules written in two days and applied unevenly to students who had done nothing wrong, which makes this a concrete record of what a rushed response to an AI risk costs an institution. What happens to the students caught is still open. The ministry's examination misconduct committee will decide on penalties before scores are released on December 11. The government and the ruling Democratic Party have suggested extending the exam ban for AI-assisted cheating to 10 years, possibly applied retroactively to this year's exam, and authorities are considering a review of past exams based on credible tips. A new minister has not been named.

Sources: The Korea Herald, Time

AidFinder, Built by a Palisades Fire Survivor, Pitches States an AI Tool to Match Disaster Survivors With Aid Programs, With No State Customer and No Users Yet

Fortune reported on Monday, November 23, on AidFinder, a Miami-based startup co-founded by Jonathon Nostrant, whose Los Angeles home was damaged in the Palisades fire of January 2025. What he found afterward, Fortune writes, was a tangle of federal, state and local assistance programs, each with its own forms, eligibility rules and deadlines. Survivors with insurance must also report their payouts to every agency they apply to, because FEMA cannot pay for losses an insurer already covers. AidFinder uses AI to match a survivor with programs they may qualify for, explain the eligibility rules and prepare the applications, and survivors review everything before it is submitted. The platform also lets residents store identification, tax records and photos of their homes in a digital vault before a disaster. The intended buyers are states, which would offer it to residents under the state's own name. "We're giving the state the ability to launch a landing page that's powered by AidFinder," Nostrant told Fortune. Fortune is direct about where this stands: "For now, it is a pitch." AidFinder has not signed its first state customer and survivors have yet to use the platform, according to a November 17 report by Refresh Miami that Fortune cites. Nostrant told that outlet the company is in talks with 30 states, including Florida, and that Craig Fugate, who ran FEMA under President Obama, has accepted a seat on its board. Why it matters: The news here is a market more than a product. Fortune reports that FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton has told state emergency managers to prepare to handle more of the recovery work themselves, and that states have faced unusually long waits for federal disaster declarations. That turns the paperwork between a survivor and the aid they qualify for into a state problem, and companies are forming to sell states an AI layer for it. This one has a credible board member and 30 conversations, and zero deployments. Nothing has been published about how accurately it matches people to programs or how its applications fare with the agencies that receive them, and no survivor has used the tool. Fortune closes on the open question: whether a state will pay to offer it.

Sources: Fortune

India's CERT-In Says Attackers Using AI Tools Pushed Unauthorized Payments Through Providers That Handle Government-Linked Payments, Cutting the Gap From Break-In to Damage From Days to a Few Hours

India's Computer Emergency Response Team, CERT-In, has warned that AI-assisted attacks have produced unauthorized financial transactions, The Hacker News reported on November 23. The affected organizations include banks and financial services firms, and also technology service providers that support government-linked payments and platforms that pay vendors and partners. MLQ.ai, which dates the advisory to Friday, November 20, and says it is rated "Critical", quotes the agency: "CERT-In has received reports of unauthorised financial transactions involving organisations outside the financial sector." According to the advisory as quoted by The Hacker News, "attackers targeted business records and approval processes to carry out unauthorized transactions through otherwise legitimate systems." They changed the records or approval relationships that decide who gets paid, then let the normal payment process run. The agency warned that multiple approvals inside the same provider's system may offer limited protection if that system is the thing that was compromised. On the AI, CERT-In says separate threat-hunting found AI-driven agentic attack frameworks used across enterprise, technology, government, academic and research sectors: automated scanning for exposed systems, rapid writing of exploits from published patches, clusters of AI agents working on intrusion tasks in parallel, and attack scripts that rewrite themselves when blocked. "Agentic tooling compressed the time between initial access and final impact from days to a few hours," the agency said. The Hacker News adds that an earlier CERT-In warning this month named a group called Crafty Falcon, and that India's cybercrime coordination center has reported multi-crore unauthorized transactions (a crore is 10 million rupees) at banks, lenders and fintech companies. Why it matters: A national cyber agency has put on record that AI-assisted intrusions have moved past banks into the service providers that run payment workflows for others, including government-linked payments, and that the attack leaves the payment system itself untouched. The money moves through approved channels because the record of who should be paid was altered first. MLQ.ai quotes the advisory on that point: "A transaction may pass the usual application and approval checks even when the records used to initiate it have been changed without authorisation." The stated speed, days compressed to hours, is the agency's own measurement. Public payment programs depend on the same outsourced approval systems. What is not known is large: CERT-In did not name the organizations, say how much money was paid out, give a count of incidents or attribute the activity to anyone.

Sources: The Hacker News, MLQ.ai (an AI-produced newsroom, used here for the advisory date and quotes only)

Google.org Puts More Than C$7 Million Into Youth Online Safety and AI Literacy in Canada, Planning 7,000 Trained Educators by 2028 on the Back of a 2023 Trial That Reached 500

Google.org, Google's philanthropic arm, announced on Friday, November 20, more than seven million Canadian dollars for youth safety, mental health and AI education across Canada, Superpower Daily reported the same day. The package supports more than a dozen nonprofits. Google says the programs are expected to reach over 550,000 young people and educators, and the report is careful on this point: "Those are projected figures, not people already reached." The largest AI education piece is Experience AI, a free program from the Raspberry Pi Foundation that gives teachers classroom resources and training, with lessons on how AI works, how it is used, and its social and ethical implications. With Canadian partners Actua and Digital Moment, it plans to train 7,000 educators by 2028, who are expected to reach an estimated 300,000 young people. One completed result sits behind the target: a 2023 Canadian trial of Experience AI with Digital Moment reached 500 educators and an estimated 56,000 young people. The new phase is 14 times that number of educators and adds Actua as a delivery partner. The rest of the money goes to digital safety outside the AI classroom: BGC Canada is running an in-person program for children aged 8 to 12 in 46 clubs, and Kids Help Phone is adding online safety and digital wellbeing lessons for grades 6 through 8. Why it matters: This is a funded, dated commitment to teach AI literacy through teachers at national scale. The structure is the notable part: the money trains educators, and the reach to young people is an estimate derived from that, both in the completed trial (500 educators, an estimated 56,000 students) and in the plan (7,000 educators, an estimated 300,000). The one hard measure available in 2028 will be the educator count. The limits of the record are plain as well: these notes rest on Superpower Daily's report alone, the funder's own announcement was not located, and no independent evaluation of the 2023 trial was found.

Sources: Superpower Daily
