#169: Impact Signals #169 — US Emergency Managers Draft Disaster Alerts With Chatbots, NOAA Funds $1.2M Pacific Cell Broadcast Rollout, Half of US School Districts Report a Cyber Incident

AI for Impact Daily Briefing, October 28, 2026

Listen on: Spotify Apple Podcasts YouTube Substack RSS

Top Stories

US Emergency Managers Are Drafting Disaster Alerts With Chatbots on Personal Accounts, and an Alabama County Already Sent an AI Hurricane Graphic That Was Not Meteorologically Accurate

Grist staff writer Jake Bittle reported on October 28, 2026, in a story co-published with WIRED, that emergency management offices across the United States are using general-purpose chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are named) to draft public warnings, even though most agencies do not formally allow generative AI for public alerts. Sarah Miller, a professor of emergency management at Pierce College in Florida and a longtime instructor at the National Disaster Preparedness Training Center, says staff are "using their own personal accounts, or they're using free ones." Rob Dale, who runs emergency management in Ingham County, Michigan, has used AI to draft practice alerts and argues "the AI is probably gonna do a better job than you are." Laura Myers, director of the University of Alabama's Center for Advanced Public Safety, took a call from an Alabama meteorologist about a hurricane graphic a county emergency manager had produced with a chatbot: "It did what he wanted it to do, but it was not meteorologically accurate." The piece sets this against a long record of human-written confusion, including adjacent California counties issuing a "voluntary" and a "mandatory" evacuation for the same 2016 wildfire, and the 2018 Montecito mudslide that killed 23 people and injured more than 160 after an alert went to residents already hit by the Thomas Fire. Hundreds of companies now market AI tools to emergency managers; one startup, EM1, trained a model on disaster researcher Jeannette Sutton's alert templates and holds contracts with about 100 government agencies and school districts, though none yet uses it to automate extreme-weather alerts. In Syracuse, New York, a public-employee union has sued the local government over an AI service answering non-emergency 911 calls. Why it matters: The story documents a gap between written policy and practice inside the offices that issue life-safety messages: most agencies have not authorized generative AI for alerts, yet the drafting is happening on personal and free accounts with no audit trail. It also fixes the shape of the vendor market, with hundreds of tools, one holding about 100 public-sector contracts, and zero automated weather alerts so far. The next verifiable step is whether any agency formally adopts an alert-drafting model rather than tolerating informal use.

Sources: Grist, WIRED

A US$1.2 Million NOAA Subaward Puts Cell Broadcast Alerts on the Roadmap for Nine Pacific Island Countries

Trade outlets reported on October 28, 2026 that the GSMA, the mobile-industry association, has received a US$1.2 million subaward from the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) to extend mobile-enabled early warning across nine Pacific island countries: the Cook Islands, Fiji, Kiribati, Nauru, Niue, Samoa, Solomon Islands, Tuvalu and Vanuatu. The work sits under the Pacific Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems Communication & Tools Project, with the GSMA co-leading the cell broadcast component alongside the COMET Program of the University Corporation for Atmospheric Research (UCAR), and the Weather Ready Pacific Program providing technical support and regional coordination. Cell broadcast lets an authorized agency push a warning to every compatible handset inside a defined area at once, bypassing the call and SMS congestion that slows networks during a disaster. The GSMA says it will prepare the technical setup and operating procedures, including who is authorized to send messages, and work with mobile operators, national disaster management offices and regulators. The funding builds on 2025 work with the Pacific Islands Telecommunications Association (PITA) and Omnitouch that produced the Pacific's first live cell broadcast test in the Solomon Islands; GSMA's program pages record rollout starting in 2025 in the Solomon Islands, Cook Islands, Samoa and Kiribati and continuing in 2026 with Niue, Tuvalu and Nauru. Kimberly Brown, head of Mobile for Humanitarian Innovation and Emerging Tech at the GSMA: "Mobile networks are a crucial channel to reach people with lifesaving information in advance of disasters." The coverage does not say which of the nine countries have alerts live today, and no completion date is given. Why it matters: This is a US weather agency funding a telecoms-industry body to build public warning plumbing in some of the most cyclone- and tsunami-exposed countries on earth, in what GSMA's own September 2026 scope document calls the largest coordinated cell broadcast rollout in the Pacific. The measurable next step is per-country: which of the nine completes a national end-user test, something only Tonga had done as of April 2026 according to the regional strategy paper.

Sources: SecurityBrief Asia, ITBrief Asia, Communications Today, GSMA

51 Percent of US School Districts Had a Cyber Incident Last Year, Two-Thirds Have One or Two Security Staff, and the New K-12 Report Says AI Adoption Is Outrunning the Rules

The Center for Internet Security (CIS), its Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center (MS-ISAC) and the Consortium for School Networking (CoSN) published the 2026 State of K-12 Cybersecurity Report on October 27, 2026, subtitled "America's Schools on the Digital Frontlines in the AI Era." StateScoop's Keely Quinlan reported the survey was fielded in August and September 2026 among 426 K-12 cybersecurity and technology leaders. The headline figure is 51 percent of schools and districts reporting a cyber incident in the past 12 months; 34 percent of those with an incident said it disrupted day-to-day operations such as instruction, student information systems, communications or payroll. Capacity is thin: 66 percent of districts have only one or two people responsible for cybersecurity, 56 percent say their programs are underfunded, 70 percent say budget constraints forced them to delay security projects or staffing, and almost 80 percent say they need additional support or guidance. Phishing or email compromise featured in 87 percent of reported incidents. On the network side, MS-ISAC's malicious-domain blocking service recorded nearly 800 billion access attempts across member districts and blocked 3.52 billion connections to known or suspected malicious destinations, an average of more than 3.1 million per protected organization. The AI finding is qualitative: the report says schools are adopting AI faster than they are writing policies and safeguards for it, and that AI makes phishing and impersonation more convincing; it recommends formal AI-use policies and vendor assessment. Michael Patton, superintendent of Saratoga Springs City School District in New York: "when it comes to a major cyber event, I worry that most of us are not well prepared." Why it matters: This is the first edition of the national K-12 report to frame itself around the AI era, and the numbers behind that frame describe staffing rather than models: half of districts breached, two-thirds with at most two security staff, and AI policy lagging adoption. The 3.52 billion blocked malicious connections set the baseline the 2027 edition will be measured against. CIS and CoSN present the findings in a webinar on November 4, 2026, at cisecurity.org.

Sources: StateScoop, Center for Internet Security

A €5.4 Million German-Kenyan Project Runs Six Maize Varieties Through 18 Greenhouses and Machine-Learning Crop Models to Breed for Stresses That Hit at the Same Time

Two Kenyan outlets reported from Siaya County on October 28, 2026 on MultiStress, a research unit funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG, listed as RU 6101) with roughly €5.4 million (TV47 reports €5.5 million) for an initial four years, 2026 to 2030, with a possible four-year extension. Jaramogi Oginga Odinga University of Science and Technology (JOOUST) is the Kenyan coordinating partner, working with the Universities of Göttingen, Tübingen and Hohenheim; Streamline Feed also names the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT), and TV47 names the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) for soil analysis. Local setup cost nearly 50 million Kenyan shillings. The premise, in the project's words, is that "climate change rarely subjects crops to a single stressor at a time," yet breeding programs typically test drought, nutrient limitation, pests and disease one by one. The team narrowed 20 market-available seed varieties to six, now grown under combined stress treatments in 18 greenhouses of 10 by 30 meters, producing 16 distinct greenhouse environments, with lab analysis roughly every two weeks. That experimental data feeds crop simulation models driven by machine learning, which adjust fertilizer and water inputs to forecast how each genotype performs under future climates, a process the researchers call "ideotyping," intended to let breeders select varieties for conditions that do not exist yet. Fifteen PhD students from Kenya, Uganda, China and Germany work on the project; Dr Issaka Abdulai, a Göttingen postdoctoral researcher, calls it "very, very international." The initial field phase is in Elgeyo Marakwet, with nationwide testing planned later. Why it matters: The project changes what a maize trial measures in East Africa, testing compound stress instead of single stress, with AI models used to extrapolate from greenhouse data to climates the region has not yet experienced. The committed numbers are concrete (six genotypes, 18 greenhouses, four funded years), so the open question is whether any variety selected this way reaches KALRO's release pipeline before the 2030 funding horizon.

Sources: Streamline Feed, TV47 Digital

The Chair of the UN Disability-Rights Committee Tells the General Assembly That AI Built Without Disabled People Can "Reproduce the Discrimination," and Asks States for Impact Assessments and Human Oversight

At the General Assembly's Third Committee on October 23, 2026, Miyeon Kim, Chair of the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, told member states that AI could improve accessibility and independent living, but that technologies developed without the participation of persons with disabilities can "reproduce the discrimination, deny opportunities, and undermine privacy and autonomy." She asked states to require accessible design, to assess AI's impact on disability rights before deployment, and to guarantee human oversight and access to remedies, adding that inclusive technologies must be affordable and available in developing countries. On military uses she said "Human control and accountability must be maintained." The same session, summarized by the UN under the headline "Disability Inclusion Cannot Wait until a Conflict Ends," heard Heba Hagrass, Special Rapporteur on the rights of persons with disabilities, say that the absence of disability-disaggregated data makes it hard to identify who is being left behind in humanitarian response. Ukraine's representative said more than 3.6 million people in Ukraine now live with disabilities. Digital Watch Observatory, the Geneva Internet Platform's monitoring service, carried the AI warning on October 27, and a daily AI-policy digest picked it up on October 28. Why it matters: The warning comes from the treaty body that reviews every state party's compliance with the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, so it signals what the committee will ask governments about in future reviews: whether AI systems in public services were built with disabled users, assessed for disability impact, and kept under human oversight. It lands two weeks after the PLOS Digital Health review that found only 6 of 93 AI-health studies for disabled people involved them in design, and names the same gap from the standard-setting side.

Sources: UN Press, Digital Watch Observatory
