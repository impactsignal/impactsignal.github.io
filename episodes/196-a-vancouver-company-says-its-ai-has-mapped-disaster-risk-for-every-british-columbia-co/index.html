UrbanLogiq Says Its AI Has Mapped Disaster Risk for Every Community in British Columbia Ahead of a January 1 Assessment Rule; the Regional District It Names Says It Was Never Contacted, and Kamloops Says It Is Still Comparing Options

Castanet Kamloops reported on Wednesday, November 25, that UrbanLogiq, a Vancouver company, is releasing an AI-powered platform this month that maps wildfire, flood and landslide risk for communities across British Columbia. The timing follows a rule. Under the province's Emergency and Disaster Management Act, municipalities and regional districts must prepare hazard, risk and vulnerability assessments under requirements that take effect on January 1, 2027, with five years to comply. The company says the platform shows exposed neighborhoods, vulnerable populations, roads that could be cut off, and residents who may need help first. It first announced the mapping in a press release dated October 22. The pitch is aimed at small places. "We use AI responsibly to do the heavy lifting once, for every community, so a village of 300 people has the same picture of its risk as a city of one million," chief executive Mark Masongsong said in the company's statement. UrbanLogiq says its first pass over all B.C. communities is complete and that the rest of Canada will follow. The platform is in beta testing and will be sold as a monthly subscription, and Castanet noted that its site, HRVA.ca, is not publicly accessible. What Castanet added is what the intended customers said. UrbanLogiq's head of corporate affairs told the outlet that Clinton, Logan Lake and the Thompson-Nicola Regional District could have a picture of their risks before next summer. The regional district's spokesperson, Colton Davies, said the district has not been contacted by UrbanLogiq and that it is unclear whether the tool would fit. He added that the requirements were introduced under the previous provincial government, that a new government has since been sworn in, and that "we are still waiting on confirmation from the provincial government on what this will look like." Ty Helgason, emergency preparedness manager for the City of Kamloops, said several tools and companies offer this work and that the city is "looking at the options available." UrbanLogiq's chief strategy officer, Michael Lee, said the company has had inquiries from dozens of municipalities, fire chiefs and local government staff. The report contains no accuracy testing of the maps and no price. Why it matters: A legal deadline for local disaster risk assessments has produced a product that claims to have done the first draft for a whole province at once, and the first independent check found that the customers it names had not been asked. The sequence is out of order: the maps exist before the province has confirmed what an acceptable assessment looks like, and before any local government has said it will use them. No municipality is reported as a customer, and no one outside the company has checked a map against a known flood or fire. The requirement takes effect on January 1, 2027, and its scope depends on what the new provincial government confirms.

Sources: Castanet Kamloops, EIN Presswire (UrbanLogiq press release)

Goa's Health Minister Says the State's Main Public Hospital Will Pilot an AI Chatbot That Takes a Patient's History Before the Doctor Does: He Puts the Saving at 45 Minutes Down to Five, and the Agreement to Start Has Not Been Signed

Goa's Health Minister, Vishwajit Rane, told the Press Trust of India on Sunday, November 22, that Goa Medical College and Hospital, the state-run teaching hospital at Bambolim, will pilot an AI chatbot called UN:BLOC, Medical Dialogues and eHealth Magazine reported on November 24. So far this is an announcement, and nothing has launched. The pilot begins after the state signs a memorandum of understanding with the health-technology firm behind the tool, and the minister gave no date for the signing or the start. He said the project carries no cost to the state government. As Rane described it, the chatbot collects a patient's detailed medical history before the consultation and arranges it in a dashboard for the clinician, which is updated as new details arrive. The system also reviews published medical literature and indexes each case against it, so that a panel of doctors can look at the history alongside the research and advise the treating clinician. The claimed benefit is time in crowded outpatient departments. "In a busy OPD, taking a detailed history can take 45 minutes to an hour. With that history already organised, the clinician can review it in about five minutes and spend more of the visit discussing the diagnosis and care plan," he said. Those figures are the minister's estimate; no pilot has run, so nothing has been measured. Rane said a statewide rollout could follow and claimed Goa could become the first Indian state to integrate AI across its whole public health system. The coverage contains no information on how patients with low literacy or no smartphone would use the chatbot, what languages it works in, who holds the patient data, or how its accuracy will be checked. eHealth Magazine noted that the pilot is meant to show how the tool fits existing clinical workflows before it is extended to other public facilities. Why it matters: A state government in India has named its main public teaching hospital as the test site for AI history-taking, the step in a consultation that consumes the most clinician time and depends most on what the patient is able to say. What exists is an announcement, a zero-cost arrangement and an unsigned agreement, so the terms that decide whether this serves patients (data ownership, language coverage and evaluation) are not yet public. The 45-minutes-to-five claim is the number the pilot would have to demonstrate. The memorandum of understanding comes first, and it has no date.

Sources: Medical Dialogues (reporting the Press Trust of India), eHealth Magazine

The University of Indianapolis Receives $8.5 Million From Lilly Endowment to Rebuild Up to 330 Courses Around AI and Open a Center Offering AI Consulting to Small Businesses and Nonprofits; the Three-Year Program Starts in January

The University of Indianapolis has received an $8.5 million grant from Lilly Endowment Inc., a private foundation based in Indianapolis, to establish a Center for Artificial Intelligence for Human Performance and expand AI education across its campus, WRTV, the Indianapolis ABC affiliate, reported on Monday, November 23. The three-year program is expected to begin in January. The grant is part of the endowment's Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education initiative, which WRTV reports is funding 35 Indiana colleges and universities. The plan has two halves. Inside the university, it will create or revise as many as 330 courses to bring AI into the curriculum, develop nine AI-focused micro-credentials, train faculty with an emphasis on critical thinking, ethical decision-making and responsible use, and introduce AI tools for academic advising, student support, transfer credit evaluation and data security. Outside it, the new center, to be housed in a renovated part of Krannert Memorial Library, will offer customized consulting to small businesses and nonprofit organizations that lack the resources or expertise to adopt AI on their own, with students working on the projects. "Higher education has a responsibility to prepare students not just to use these new tools, but to think critically about how and why they are used," university president Tanuja Singh said in a statement. Provost Chris Plouff said the center pairs faculty expertise with students' problem-solving "to address the real-world challenges facing Hoosier businesses and organizations." The announcement does not say how many nonprofits the center expects to serve, what the consulting will cost them, or how the grant is divided between the campus work and the outside services. The university plans its next AI Summit for fall 2027. Why it matters: Philanthropic money for AI in one US state is arriving at individual campuses with a public-service obligation attached. This grant pays for a standing place, staffed by a university, where a nonprofit with no technical staff can get AI help. The scale is specific ($8.5 million, three years, up to 330 courses), and the university is one of 35 institutions funded under the same initiative, so Indiana is building this capacity across its higher-education system at once. Nothing has been delivered yet. The program starts in January, and the announcement leaves open how many organizations the center will reach.

Sources: WRTV Indianapolis, University of Indianapolis
