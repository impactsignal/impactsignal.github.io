Top Stories

South Korea Starts Connecting Regional Public Hospitals to Shared Medical AI, With 72 Planned by 2029

South Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare launched a pilot it calls the Public Healthcare AI Expressway on Wednesday, November 11, linking Kangwon National University Hospital in Chuncheon with Yeongwol Medical Center, a smaller regional hospital. The two demonstrated one full pathway online that day: a child Yeongwol cannot treat is transferred, has surgery, is discharged and is referred back. Three AI tools run inside both hospitals' electronic medical record screens, drafting transfer requests and return letters, the pre-anesthesia assessment, and the discharge summary; the ministry estimates the transfer paperwork drops from more than 10 minutes to under one. Everything the AI writes is a draft: the attending physician makes the clinical judgment, edits the document and applies the electronic signature. Eight hospitals are in the proof of concept, 27 more are due to join for up to 35, and the stated goal is 72 public hospitals, including municipal medical centers and Red Cross hospitals, by 2029. Why it matters: Medical AI in Korea has been built and used mainly by large Seoul-area hospitals with the data, computing power and specialist staff; regional public hospitals could not afford their own. Here a national government delivers validated models as shared infrastructure, bundled with a dedicated network, national GPU computing and a standard platform, and keeps the physician's signature as the point of responsibility. No effect on patients or transfer times has been shown, and neither report gives a program cost: the only number is the ministry's own estimate of minutes saved per document. At a roundtable the same day, clinicians asked for standardized record and imaging interfaces and simpler signature steps, and the ministry said it will reflect that in the 2027 design and budget. The proof of concept runs through March 2027 and the main project is due to start in April 2027.

Sources: ChosunBiz, JKN (carrying Yonhap)

Young People Who Confide in AI Chatbots Can List the Flaws and Use Them Anyway, a Rithm Project Interview Study Finds

The 74 reported on Tuesday, November 10, on a new report from The Rithm Project, a nonprofit that studies human connection in the age of AI. It follows the group's March survey of 2,383 people aged 13 to 24, in which 18 percent said they use AI for personal and emotional support and 15 percent talk to AI characters and personas. Researchers went back to those two groups and analyzed 29 in-depth interviews with participants aged 16 to 24. None set out to confide in a chatbot: they started with homework help, grammar checks or translating an MRI report, and in almost every case the shift came at what the researchers call a gateway moment, usually a rupture with a person such as a friendship that ended or a forgotten birthday. Many could recite the flaws (flattery, made-up answers, telling users what they want to hear) and kept using it because, in the report's words, AI does not have to be trustworthy, it has to be less risky than a real person who might gossip or judge. Roughly one in four said they had little or no awareness of problem behavior. Why it matters: The earlier survey sized the group at about one in five; this report describes the route, and it runs through the general chatbot a student already uses for schoolwork, at a moment of rejection. Most current rules on companion chatbots target products built to act as friends, which is a different doorway. Michael Robb, head of research at Common Sense Media, told The 74 that how an AI product interacts with people is built into the product, so a teenager may be weighing a risk they can see, a friend judging them, against one they cannot. Interviewees also said adult guidance arrives as a flat "don't use it" or "go all in", with nothing in between. The evidence is 29 self-selected interviews, which the authors state are not representative.

Sources: The 74

Experts Say the Mystic Alerts Act Will Help Only at the Margins: Carriers Can Opt Out, and the Camp Mystic Warning Was Never Sent

The Mystic Alerts Act, signed on October 22, directs the Federal Communications Commission, working with the Department of Homeland Security and FEMA, to set requirements for mobile carriers to deliver wireless emergency alerts over satellite when cell towers are damaged or out of range. It is named for Camp Mystic, the Texas summer camp where the July 2025 Hill Country floods killed campers and counselors. On Friday, November 13, StateScoop reported what the law leaves out. Carriers are not required to offer satellite alerts; those that opt out must tell their customers. "Honestly, I think the benefit is going to be really marginal," warning systems consultant Art Botterell said, noting that the Kerr County sheriff's office did not send wireless emergency alerts during the disaster: "The bottlenecks weren't at the technology layer, they were at the procedural layer, the human factors layer and the policy layer." Elizabeth Reddy of the Colorado School of Mines said satellite could add resilience only if it is built into the tools emergency managers already use: "New technology doesn't help anybody if it isn't usable." This story has no AI in it; it is disaster-resilience reporting, the other half of the show's beat. Why it matters: The law answers a real failure, an alert that cannot reach a phone with no signal, and the people closest to the problem say that was not the failure that cost lives in Kerr County. The FCC must publish a proposed rule within six months of enactment and a final rule within 18 months, and the rule takes effect at least three years after that, which puts any requirement at roughly 2031 and only on carriers that choose to take part. Botterell pointed out that carriers are being asked to choose before the rules defining the service exist. Staffing, procedure and alerting tools that work together are not in this law; a 2025 FEMA grant program put $40 million toward state alert and warning systems, and observers questioned whether that was enough.

Sources: StateScoop, KSAT

The IRS Is Piloting an AI Agent That Builds the Fraud Case and Hands It to an Employee Before Any Decision

The Internal Revenue Service is piloting an agentic AI tool for the routine steps in identity theft and fraud cases, Jim Clifford, director of return integrity and compliance services in the IRS Taxpayer Service Division, said on Friday, November 13, at ACT-IAC's Service Delivery and Experience Summit and in an interview with FedScoop. The agency already uses machine learning to flag anomalies in returns; the slow part comes after, when a taxpayer writes back and an employee has to organize the correspondence and read every page. "What we want the agentic AI to do is review the response, summarize it, build a case, and hand it off to an employee at the point where it's really ready to make a decision," Clifford said, adding that judgments will always belong to a person. He put the scale at approximately 1 billion attempts a month by fraudsters to obtain unauthorized information; the Government Accountability Office estimates the federal government loses between $116 billion and $304 billion a year to tax fraud. Why it matters: For a person whose identity was used to file a false return, the wait is in the back office while an employee works through the paper, and this pilot aims at that step with a stated line: the AI assembles the file and a person decides. It is one official's description, with no accuracy figures, case counts, vendor or deployment date; Clifford said it would be scaled to other parts of the IRS "once it's tested and proven to be successful." The same week, CDC and FDA officials told a Washington panel on November 12 that human judgment remains essential in health emergency response, per MeriTalk.

Sources: FedScoop, MeriTalk
