Dublin City Council Switches On an AI Flood Warning Pilot, With 13 to 21 Hours of Notice Shown Only on Past Floods and Staff Reviewing Every Alert

Dublin City Council is starting a pilot of an AI early warning system for major floods this week, The Journal, The Irish Times and Silicon Republic reported on Monday, November 16, 2026. The model was built by EMR Integrated Solutions, an engineering firm in Dunboyne, County Meath, that already runs the council's flood monitoring network, and it reads river levels, rainfall, tide data and data from Met Éireann, the national weather service, looking for the patterns that have come before serious flooding. EMR says trials on historical events showed the potential for warnings 13 to 21 hours ahead of certain mountain rain-driven events; none of the three reports gives a false-alarm rate or says how many past floods were replayed. The reports also differ on details: The Journal and The Irish Times say the model draws on 10 years of sensor data while Silicon Republic says EMR has operated the network for nine years, and The Irish Times says trials have run in the city for several months while the other two describe tests against historical events. Every alert is reviewed by council personnel, and all decisions on flood protection and public alerts stay with council staff. The backdrop, per The Irish Times, is Storm Chandra earlier this year, when the Phoenix Park recorded 40 millimetres of rain in one day, two-thirds of its January average, and there had been no rainfall warning for Dublin in advance. Climate minister Darragh O'Brien said, "The single most valuable thing we can give people is time." Why it matters: An Irish local authority is running a machine-learning flood model in live operations for a full winter, and the headline lead time is a vendor result from replayed events, so this winter is the first test against floods that have not happened yet and the first chance to count missed events and false alarms. The design choice on record is that the model only raises a flag and a named public body decides. The pilot runs through the autumn and winter, and both the minister and EMR's chief commercial officer, Mark O'Connor, have tied any extension to other catchments to its results.

Sources: The Journal, The Irish Times, Silicon Republic

Stanford's Review of 52 Causal Studies Finds Classroom AI Helps Teachers More Than Students, and Students Who Rely on It Do Worse Once It Is Removed

The AI Hub for Education, part of Stanford University's SCALE Initiative, released a review of 52 studies of AI in educational settings on Tuesday, November 10, 2026, Chalkbeat reported on Friday, November 13. It is the hub's second review and adds 32 new studies, every one a randomized controlled trial or a quasi-experimental design; Chalkbeat notes it is a synthesis and not a meta-analysis, because the tools and uses differ too much to pool. Collectively the studies suggest students do not learn much when they use AI to produce essays or solve problems, and when those students later work without AI they do worse than other students. AI looks better for explaining concepts and for practice, but the results there are mixed or show little gain over traditional study, and education-specific tools were not necessarily better than general-purpose chatbots. Teachers come out ahead: those who let AI do the first pass on grading gave more feedback and more detailed feedback, and teachers saved time on lesson plans without a drop in quality, though students who believed a lesson was AI-generated reported being less motivated, and in one study from Turkey students did worse when teachers who were already lower-performing used AI for lesson plans. Susanna Loeb, SCALE's faculty director and an author of the report, said of districts that will not redesign classwork around the tools: "I wouldn't use them. On their own without thought, they have some negative effects on learning." Why it matters: The pool of strong causal evidence on classroom AI grew by 32 studies in one update and points the same way as the smaller pool did: the measurable benefit so far sits with teacher workload and feedback, and the finding that students did worse without the tool only shows up when a study tests them after it is removed. The gap the authors name is specific. Chalkbeat reports there is still very little research from US K-12 classrooms, with many of the studies coming from other countries or involving college students.

Sources: Chalkbeat

Malaysia Will Test AI Fall Detection in Up to 50 Homes of Older People Living Alone, With Radar and Thermal Sensors and No Cameras

Malaysia's government announced on Sunday, November 15, 2026, a six-month pilot at Razak Mansion, a housing estate in Kuala Lumpur, that will fit up to 50 homes with sensors to detect when an older resident living alone has fallen or has stopped moving for an unusual length of time, Malay Mail reported. The system combines AI with radar and thermal sensors and uses no cameras, so residents are not visually recorded in their homes; alerts go through internet-connected devices to caregivers and security personnel. This is an announcement, not a running system: Communications Minister Fahmi Fadzil said the project is still in design and preparation, with procurement of devices next and a start expected early next year, after which it runs around the clock for six months. "We want to make sure that it is safe, privacy-first and sustainable, because it has to be cost-effective for rollout to other places as well," he said. Federal Territories Minister Hannah Yeoh said Razak Mansion was chosen because more than 60 percent of its residents are older adults, and acknowledged that some may hesitate to take part because two government agencies are involved. Why it matters: A national government has committed to testing in-home monitoring of older people under conditions it stated in advance: no cameras, a fixed six-month window, a cap of 50 homes, and cost named as a pass or fail question. The minister's own remark about hesitancy points to what the pilot will show first, which is whether residents accept sensors from two government agencies in their homes at all. Procurement comes next, then a start early in 2027, and both ministers have tied any expansion to the findings.

Sources: Malay Mail

Japan Plans an Asia-Pacific Early Warning Network for COP31, Built on Operating Experience From Six Southeast Asian Countries

Japan plans to launch a framework to speed up early warning systems for weather disasters in developing Asian countries, Kyodo News reported on Monday, November 16, 2026, citing a government source. The source said arrangements are being made for Environment Minister Hiroyoshi Sasagawa to unveil it at the ministers' meeting of COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, which Kyodo says is set to begin on Tuesday, November 17. Kyodo describes a network meant to promote the introduction of technologies in developing countries and the sharing of operational case studies, drawing on high-accuracy forecasting being developed by private companies, with Asia as a model region before any global expansion. Jiji Press, in a report carried by The Japan Times, adds that Japan began a public-private project in 2023 to spread early warning services in the Asia-Pacific, that the Japanese firm Weathernews provides forecasts and warnings of sudden localized downpours in Thailand, and that the government aims to share knowledge and examples from six ASEAN countries. Neither report names a budget, a list of member countries or a start date, and neither describes specific AI systems; both describe systems that analyze satellite and radar data and issue alerts. Kyodo gives the reason for the timing: record rainfall and flooding in Thailand from mid-September had led to 83 deaths and affected 2.2 million households as of late October, according to the ASEAN Coordinating Centre for Humanitarian Assistance on disaster management. Why it matters: If the plan is unveiled as described, a country with one of the most developed warning systems in the region moves from selling individual forecasting services to organizing a standing network for sharing how those systems are run day to day. As of today it rests on unnamed government sources, with no money, membership or timeline stated. The unveiling is expected at the COP31 ministers' meeting this week, and the conference is scheduled to close on November 20.

Sources: Kyodo News, Jiji Press via The Japan Times
