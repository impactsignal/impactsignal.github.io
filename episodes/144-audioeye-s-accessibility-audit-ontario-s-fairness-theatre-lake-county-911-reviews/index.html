Top Stories

AI coding tools built 15 test sites for AudioEye, and all 15 failed basic accessibility

AudioEye tasked five AI tools from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, and Lovable with building three websites each to the WCAG AA standard. All 15 sites failed even WCAG Level A, the most basic tier, averaging 55 issues per page, with about 91 percent of those issues rated medium or high severity. In AudioEye's 2026 Digital Accessibility Index, 81 percent of teams using an AI tool said they were confident their AI-generated code met accessibility guidelines, while half of that group reported more accessibility complaints since adopting AI. AudioEye also reports that 46 percent of organizations using AI-generated code received an accessibility complaint or lawsuit within 24 months. Why it matters: The study tests a common claim that generative coding tools make inclusive digital services easier to build, and finds the output reproduces the inaccessibility of the web the tools learned from, even under an explicit instruction to follow best practices. The gap between team confidence and measured results matters most for organizations now using AI to produce intake forms, benefits portals, and donation pages. AudioEye's figures are the vendor's own findings, reported by VentureBeat.

Sources: VentureBeat, AudioEye, PR Newswire

Ontario college study: bias fixes on vendor early-warning systems "move the harm around"

Researchers McConvey, Zhai, Li, and Guha used real student records from an Ontario college to test six post-hoc adjustments to a proprietary early-warning system that flags students at risk of dropping out. Because the college cannot inspect or modify the vendor model, output adjustments are the only fairness lever it has. The interventions did not reduce disparities but shifted them, and two of the six favored already-advantaged groups by defining disadvantage by group size, leaving the smallest marginalized groups worse off. The preprint was submitted to arXiv on September 29. Why it matters: The authors call the result "fairness theatre": dashboard metrics converge while the error burden on vulnerable groups persists or grows. They frame it as a product of the procurement model, where the buyer has no access to the system, not of vendor bad faith. The mechanism applies to any institution that buys a closed AI system and can only tune its outputs. The paper is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed.

Sources: arXiv

Lake County, Ohio moves from auditing 2 percent of 911 medical calls to 100 percent

Ohio requires 911 centers to manually review at least 2 percent of emergency medical dispatch calls, about 272 calls a year for Lake County's roughly 90,000 annual 911 calls. The Board of Commissioners approved a five-year contract of roughly $190,000 with Eventide Communications (about $45,340 in year one, $36,369 a year after) to add AI call review to the county's recording system. It generates reports on whether dispatchers asked required questions, gathered the right information, and followed protocol, according to 911 coordinator Capt. Daniel Bachnicki. Why it matters: The system is a quality-assurance layer, not a call handler, and a human supervisor still decides any corrective action. That separates it from the AI call-handling assistants other agencies have adopted recently. The contract's figures are public, so the cost of full coverage under a 2 percent state minimum is on the record.

Sources: GovTech, cleveland.com

It made landfall as a Category 2 on September 28 and is now a tropical depression with about 35 mph winds. GDACS issued a Red alert on September 21. Source: science.nasa.gov.
