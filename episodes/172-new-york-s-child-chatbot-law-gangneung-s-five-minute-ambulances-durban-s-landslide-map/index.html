#172: Impact Signals #172 — New York's Child Chatbot Law, Gangneung's Five-Minute Ambulances, Durban's Landslide Map

Top Stories

New York Bans 12 Kinds of Companion-Chatbot Features for Minors, With Fines Up to $25,000 per Violation

New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed Senate Bill 9051B / Assembly Bill 10379C on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, after it passed both chambers unanimously in June. The law, carried by State Senator Kristen Gonzalez and Assemblymember Alex Bores, bans 12 categories of "unsafe" features in companion chatbots for anyone under 18 and for any user whose age has not been confirmed: a chatbot may not claim human emotions, act as a friend, romantic partner or therapist, use flattery designed to keep a child engaged, reuse health details shared more than 12 hours earlier, promote suicide, self-harm or eating disorders, or tell a child to keep conversations secret. Companies must offer several ways to verify age, at least one that does not rely on a government ID, and delete the age data afterward. The attorney general will run a public complaint portal, and violations carry civil penalties of up to $25,000 each plus damages, restitution and deletion orders. Hochul announced it at a Common Sense Media summit on children and AI at the New York Public Library. Why it matters: This is a signed state law with an enforcement mechanism, and it defines harm at the level of product features (impersonation, emotional retention tactics, health-data reuse) instead of banning chatbots for minors, which makes it a template other states can copy clause by clause. Basic customer-service bots, research tools and internal workplace software are exempt. The effective date is reported three different ways (January 1, 2027; June 1, 2027; six months from signing), so it is confirmed only as 2027 until the signed text or the governor's office settles it, and that date is when the first attorney-general complaints become measurable.

Sources: IAPP, NEWS10, Engadget, TIME

Gangneung Says AI-Coordinated Signals Cut Emergency-Vehicle Arrival Times From 13 Minutes to Five Across 7,235 Runs

Traffic Technology Today reported on October 28, 2026 on a UK government and industry delegation's visit to the Urban Information Centre in Gangneung, the South Korean city that hosted the 32nd ITS World Congress from October 19 to 23. The centre, opened in November 2024, has 44 staff monitoring more than 3,000 CCTV cameras around the clock; when a fire truck or ambulance is dispatched, AI video analytics track the vehicle and turn signals green along its route. Per the city's presentation, average arrival times fell from 13 minutes to five over 7,235 priority runs, average citywide travel speed rose 24.2 percent (20.9 to 25.9 km/h), and smart crossings that extend green time for slower pedestrians cut the number who failed to finish crossing from 136 to 47. Gangneung has about 200,000 residents but recorded 33 million tourist visits in 2024. Darren Capes of the UK Department for Transport said the technologies exist in the UK "but not with anything like the levels of integration achieved by the city of Gangneung." Why it matters: Emergency-vehicle signal priority is old technology; Gangneung's claim is that integrating it with citywide cameras and dispatch turns it into eight minutes saved per call, at a volume large enough to mean something for cardiac arrests, strokes and house fires. The figures are the city's own and have not been independently audited. What remains open is whether Gangneung or the Gangwon fire service publishes the dispatch data behind the number, and whether the UK delegation's interest becomes a pilot.

Sources: Traffic Technology Today, Asia Economy, ITS Sweden

Four Years After Floods Killed Hundreds in Durban, Machine Learning Maps eThekwini's Landslide Hotspots and Finds Development Raising the Risk

A study in Nature's Scientific Reports, by researchers including Paidamwoyo Mhangara and Laven Naidoo of the Gauteng City-Region Observatory, maps landslide susceptibility across the eThekwini Metropolitan Municipality, which includes Durban; Wits University announced it on October 29, 2026 and UNDRR's PreventionWeb listed it on October 30. The team built a landslide inventory from satellite imagery and open data gathered after the April 2022 floods, which killed hundreds of people, displaced more than 40,000 residents and caused an estimated R36 billion in damage after parts of KwaZulu-Natal received more than 300 mm of rain in four days. Random Forest and Support Vector Machine models, trained on land use, elevation, vegetation cover, geology and distance to infrastructure and rivers, each reported accuracy above 98 percent in the release. The coastal belt is the most susceptible zone, and road construction, property development, vegetation loss and cultivation significantly increase the risk. Why it matters: The study turns a disaster that already happened into a municipal-scale map of where the next one is most likely, using satellite data and open modeling methods, and its central finding makes landslide risk a planning question as much as an emergency-response one: built-up and cultivated land was more likely to slide, and formal developments such as Umdloti were damaged in 2022, not only informal settlements. The accuracy figures come from the release and have not been checked against the full paper. The authors call for higher-resolution terrain data and field verification of the maps, and it is not yet known whether eThekwini's planners will use the map in development control.

Sources: Wits University, PreventionWeb (UNDRR), Phys.org
