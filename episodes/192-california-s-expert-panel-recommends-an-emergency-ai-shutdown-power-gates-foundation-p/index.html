California's Expert Panel Recommends Giving the State Power to Order an AI System Shut Down in an Emergency and Cutting Incident Reporting From 15 Days to 48 Hours

This is a new development in a story this show reported on September 19, when California's governor signed an executive order giving state officials and outside experts until November 16 to recommend changes to the state's AI safety laws. The recommendations were released on Wednesday, November 18, and they are proposals, not law. According to the Governor's Office, companies would have to develop verified risk-containment protocols to shut down their models if needed, "giving the state authority to order an immediate shutdown in an emergency." CalMatters, the nonprofit newsroom that covers the state capitol, reports that the shutdown power would apply when a company refuses to act to contain a system that goes wrong, and lists the rest of the package: license the independent auditors who test AI models and revoke the licenses of those who lose their independence; consider having state officials, not the AI companies, assign auditors to evaluations; shorten the window for reporting critical safety incidents to 48 hours from 15 days; extend whistleblower protections to contractors and former employees; and require developers of large systems to demonstrate that they can shut a model down. The Governor's Office says the state's Government Operations Agency consulted the California Office of Emergency Services, the state's disaster agency, in developing the report, and Anadolu Agency reports that after any shutdown that office would investigate the incident and update its guidance. The panel had four members: Alondra Nelson of the Institute for Advanced Study, Rob Reich of Stanford University, Gillian Hadfield of Johns Hopkins University and Jason Goldman, a former Google and Twitter executive. Reich's line in the release: "Today there is more oversight of the cafeteria offering sandwiches to employees at a frontier AI developer than of the AI models they design." Why it matters: The September order has produced its deliverable, and it is specific. A US state has on paper a design for an emergency shutdown power over AI systems, a 48-hour reporting clock in place of 15 days, and state-licensed auditors, with its disaster agency named in the process, which places AI incidents in the same administrative frame as other emergencies a state manages. None of it binds anyone yet. CalMatters reports the scope limit as well: California law defines catastrophic risk as a single event capable of killing more than 50 people or causing $1 billion in theft or property damage, and the report addresses only that category. Automated discrimination, job loss and harms to children's safety and mental health are outside it; the co-authors argue that a system of credentialed auditors built for catastrophic risk will make other evaluations easier. Legislators can introduce bills when they return to Sacramento on December 7, and the incoming governor said in a written statement that he will work with the Legislature to put "full incident reporting and real shutdown authority into law."

Sources: Office of the Governor of California, CalMatters (via Long Beach Post), Anadolu Agency, StateScoop

Gates Foundation Grants Exo $11.6 Million for a Handheld Ultrasound Whose AI Runs on the Device With No Internet Connection

Exo, a California ultrasound company, announced on Thursday, November 19, that it has received an $11.6 million grant from the Gates Foundation. According to the company's release, the money will support development of "an affordable, multi-use handheld ultrasound system that enables frontline healthcare workers without specialized ultrasound expertise to acquire and interpret images for essential diagnostics and risk screening," aimed at low- and middle-income countries. Exo's platform pairs silicon-based imaging hardware with AI that guides the user in capturing an image and interpreting it in real time, and the release states that "the AI runs directly on the device, without requiring an internet connection." The company lists the barriers it is aiming at: equipment cost, the difficulty of imaging, shortages of specialists and unreliable connectivity. Chief executive Sandeep Akkaraju thanked the foundation for its "continued support," wording that Inside Philanthropy's November 20 funding roundup also notes, which indicates this is not the foundation's first grant to the company. Medical Device Network, a trade publication, reported the grant on November 20 and set it against a figure it attributes to a 2026 World Bank report: almost two-thirds of people globally cannot reliably access ultrasound imaging. Why it matters: A major global health funder has placed a specific bet: $11.6 million on ultrasound that a health worker without imaging training can operate, with the AI on the device so that it works where there is no connection. That targets two constraints at once, the shortage of trained sonographers and the lack of reliable internet. The limits are plain. The grant funds development, not a product in clinics, and no evidence has been published in the release or the coverage that untrained users reach accurate readings with the device. No duration, target countries, price point, clinical validation results or deployment date appear either. The company gave no timeline, so the next step is not on the record.

Sources: Medical Device Network, Inside Philanthropy, Exo (via Business Wire)

Anthropic's Nasdaq Listing Puts a Price on Its Giving Pledges, $112 Billion From the Founders at the Offer Price, and Nonprofits Prepare for Money That Mostly Cannot Move Until Spring

This is a new development in a story this show reported on October 19, when the AI company Anthropic said it would put 5 percent of its shares into a public benefit fund. The company's shares began trading on the Nasdaq exchange on Thursday, November 19, and the Associated Press reported on Friday that the listing valued the company at a record $1.5 trillion. According to the AP, the company still plans to dedicate 5 percent of its shares to the new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, and its seven co-founders say they will give away 80 percent of their equity, "a $112 billion commitment based on its IPO price." The AP's reporting is about how long nonprofits expect to wait. "Nonprofits aren't counting on an immediate charitable windfall," it writes: many of the company's 5,300 employees cannot sell or gift the bulk of their shares until next spring. GiveDirectly, the nonprofit that sends cash to poor households by mobile phone, has spent months identifying the biggest barriers to growing quickly, its president and chief executive Nick Allardice told the AP. Recipients need a phone to receive a transfer, and GiveDirectly offers one for about $15, usually subtracted from the first payment, so the organization secured investments this year to build a supply chain for many more phones. "I have this level of cautious optimism," Allardice said. "But I can't foresee the future, and I don't know exactly what's going to happen." A separate TechCrunch estimate on November 19 added up three channels: the benefit fund (almost 850 million shares, worth about $80 billion at a trading price of about $95), employee pledges it estimates at $23 billion, and a company match it estimates at roughly $53 billion, for a total of $156 billion against the $617 billion Americans gave to charity in 2025. The AP and TechCrunch figures are counted differently and do not add together. Why it matters: The shares now have a market price, so pledges reported in October as percentages can be stated in dollars, and at least one large operating nonprofit says on the record that it has changed its own logistics in anticipation. None of it is cash in a charity's account. The Chronicle of Philanthropy reported on October 29 that the company's securities filing says the founders' 80 percent pledge is not binding and that the benefit fund is a limited liability company, a structure that requires less public disclosure of grants than a foundation. TechCrunch notes that employees have to make good on their pledges, that the value of the match depends on the share price when it is delivered, and that shares may go into donor-advised funds, which would slow their effect. Neither outlet reports where the benefit fund's money will go beyond a first $200 million commitment, cited by TechCrunch, to study the effect of AI on workers, and the causes named by the people interviewed are global health, extreme poverty, animal welfare and AI safety; humanitarian response is not singled out. "The challenge is that deploying philanthropic capital is hard," Kumar Garg, president of Renaissance Philanthropy, told the AP. The end of the employee share lockup in the spring is the first point at which large gifts of stock become possible.

Sources: Associated Press (via ABC News and KIRO 7), TechCrunch, Chronicle of Philanthropy

Somali Researchers Propose Judging AI Outbreak Alerts by the Action That Follows, Citing 13,737 Alerts in Nigeria's Borno State of Which 16 Needed a Response

A review paper published on Tuesday, November 17, in the journal Frontiers in Public Health looks at AI outbreak warnings from the other end: what a health system can do once the alert arrives. Its title is "Beyond prediction speed: evaluating the data-to-action gap in AI-based outbreak surveillance in conflict-affected health systems," and its seven authors are based at Somali National University, Salaam University and the Benaadir Research, Consultancy and Evaluation Center, all in Mogadishu. It is a different paper from the 33-study review this show covered on November 19. The abstract says improved predictive performance "does not necessarily translate into effective public health action." Before the alert, the data can be thin: in Southwest Cameroon, the share of cholera surveillance reports filed on time was 16.3 percent during high-intensity conflict and 40.7 percent when conflict eased. After the alert, the workload is in sorting. In Borno State, Nigeria, a conventional (not AI) Early Warning, Alert and Response System generated 13,737 alerts, and 89.6 percent were verified within 24 hours. Of the verified alerts, 9,794 were discarded, 2,501 were monitored and 16 required a response. For AI specifically, the authors cite one study of an outbreak prediction method that reported a sensitivity of 0.72, meaning it caught about 72 percent of outbreaks, with roughly 2.8 to 3.6 false alerts per local government area per year. From that they propose four stages for evaluating any AI alert system: data readiness, alert performance, verification and decision-making, and public health action and equity. Why it matters: The yardstick comes from researchers inside one of the health systems in question, and the Borno figures put a number on the work an alert creates: 13,737 alerts sorted to find 16 that needed action. An AI system that raises more alerts earlier adds to that verification load unless it is also more precise, and the one AI figure the paper cites carries about three false alerts per district per year. The authors are direct about the paper's status. It is a mini review that tests no system, the framework "is conceptual and has not yet been prospectively validated," and it "should not be interpreted as an established causal pathway." They also note that direct evidence linking AI-generated outbreak alerts to better health outcomes is limited, and that the Borno and Cameroon figures come from conventional surveillance. The authors call for prospective validation and report none under way.

Sources: Frontiers in Public Health, Bioengineer.org
