A Raleigh Landlord's AI Agent Told a Tenant in Writing She Could Pay $3,934 in Back Rent on November 30, and She Was Evicted on November 19

Laura Mack rents at Colony Townhome Apartments in Raleigh, North Carolina, and was behind on rent. On November 8 she received an email signed "Colony Townhome Apartments" that asked, "What date works for you to pay the full $3,934.17?" She answered that she would like to pay on the last day of the month, and a reply the same day said, "Paying on November 30, the last day of the month, works." WRAL Investigates, which first reported the case on Thursday, November 19, reviewed the emails. Mack told WRAL she assumed a person in the leasing office had written to her, because the messages included her personal details and mentioned a bathroom ceiling leak that had taken almost two weeks to repair. On Monday, November 16, she received an eviction notice. When she called the office, she says, a leasing agent told her both November 8 emails had been written by Olivia, the company's AI agent, and that Olivia had granted the extension by mistake. The complex told residents in December 2025 that Olivia would handle rent and maintenance questions around the clock. WRAL reports that Olivia's emails are usually clearly marked, and that these two were not. The newer facts come from WRAL's follow-up, carried by stations across the country on November 24. Mack went to the Wake County courthouse on Thursday, November 19, to try to reverse the notice, and the follow-up reports that she was evicted that day. She told WRAL she accepts that her rent was overdue, and her complaint is about the promise: "If you told me till the 30th, I'll get out by the 30th." North Carolina State Senator Jim Burgin told WRAL the November 8 emails should have said they were written by the AI agent, and that the landlord will have to defend the fact that its bot gave her the extension in writing. "Before someone evicted that lady, they should have reviewed their documents," he said. Why it matters: This is a documented case, with the emails on record, of an AI agent making a commitment about housing that its operator then did not honor, and of a person losing her home in the gap. Two facts carry the weight. The messages were not labeled as coming from an AI, so the tenant had no reason to doubt them. And the landlord's explanation, as the tenant recounts it, treats the agent's written answer as a mistake that binds no one. The amounts are small and the case is one tenant, but an unlabeled agent answering for an institution about money and deadlines is the same arrangement being sold to benefits offices and aid programs. Whether a company is held to what its agent tells a customer is still open. A spokesperson for the complex said on Friday, November 20, that its owners are looking into the eviction, and no court ruling on the dispute has been reported.

Sources: WRAL News, WRAL via CNN Newsource (carried by Hawaii News Now), TheGrio

Weather and Farm Agencies From 15 Countries Spend a Week in Abu Dhabi Learning to Run and Check AI Weather Models Themselves, With a Target of 30 Countries by 2027

National meteorological and agricultural authorities from 15 countries met in Abu Dhabi from November 9 to 13 for a hands-on course in using AI weather models for farm advisories, the World Meteorological Organization reported on Monday, November 23. The program runs under an initiative called AIM for Scale and was convened by the International Affairs Office at the UAE Presidential Court. It was hosted at the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence and the UAE's National Center of Meteorology, with support from the University of Chicago. This was the second year. The 2025 pilot had six countries: Bangladesh, Chile, Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria and the United Arab Emirates. This year Bolivia, Colombia, Indonesia, Pakistan, Peru, the Philippines, Rwanda, Senegal and Sierra Leone joined them. Delegations paired staff from national weather services with staff from agriculture ministries. According to WMO, participants learned to run AI forecast models on their own, benchmark them, check their output against observations, and adapt a global forecast to local conditions. Each delegation was meant to leave with what the organizers call a minimum viable product, a first version of a farm-weather service to test at home. Devdiscourse, reporting on November 24, describes WMO's part as keeping the AI work tied to the observation networks and standards that weather services already use, including what data a team can use to verify a forecast. Why it matters: The cohort went from six countries to fifteen in a year, and the course is built around national agencies evaluating AI weather models themselves. That places the judgment about when a model is good enough for a planting or harvest advisory inside the national service that has to stand behind it. The reports show no results yet. Neither account says how many of the six 2025 countries now run an AI-based advisory for farmers, how many farmers receive one, or how accurate the models proved in any country, and the funding amount is not stated. WMO is also a partner in the program it describes. The stated target of 30 countries by 2027 is the next thing that can be checked, along with whether any of the 15 prototypes becomes a service farmers actually receive.

Sources: World Meteorological Organization, Devdiscourse

Morocco Begins Field Tests of MA-INDAR, a National Phone Alert System, With Six Provinces Scheduled Through Thursday and Service Expected in December

Morocco's Ministry of Interior announced on Monday, November 23, a national mobile warning system called MA-INDAR, Morocco World News and Hespress reported the same day. The system sends alerts to mobile phones inside a specific geographic area affected by a threat. It uses two channels: ordinary SMS, and Cell Broadcast, which delivers a text with a distinctive sound to compatible phones. According to the ministry, messages will state the nature of the danger, its severity and its expected timing, along with instructions for residents, and the system will be used only in exceptional circumstances tied to risk management and emergency response. The ministry says a first phase of technical testing is complete and a second phase of field trials under real conditions starts this week. The first tests are scheduled for Tuesday, November 24, between 11 a.m. and noon in Sidi Ayad, in Midelt Province, and Azemmour, in El Jadida Province, with a possible repeat on Wednesday. On Thursday, November 26, trials are scheduled in Taourirt and Khenifra provinces from 11 a.m. to 1 p.m., and in El Hajeb and Ifrane provinces from 2 p.m. to 4 p.m. The ministry stressed that these are technical exercises, that they do not signal any real danger, and that people who receive a test message do not need to do anything. Public information campaigns are planned alongside the tests. No artificial intelligence component is described: this is alert delivery infrastructure. Why it matters: Morocco now has a named national phone alert system with a public test schedule and a stated month for going live. The country's recent disasters, including the September 2023 earthquake in the High Atlas, struck without any way to push a warning or instruction to every phone in the affected area. The pairing of two channels is the design choice to note: Cell Broadcast reaches compatible phones at once without a subscriber list, and SMS covers handsets that cannot receive it. Neither report gives a cost, names the telecom operators or equipment suppliers involved, or says what share of phones in Morocco can receive Cell Broadcast. This is the second national alert rollout on the show in a week, after Malaysia's on November 18, and the first from North Africa. The ministry expects MA-INDAR to enter service during December, after the tests are completed and the results assessed, so the outcome of the six-province trials is the next verifiable fact.

Sources: Morocco World News, Hespress English

FEMA Asks Vendors for AI Tools That Flag High-Risk Businesses in Its Grant Programs, and Writes the Limit Into the Request: No Live Screening and No Eligibility Decisions

The Federal Emergency Management Agency is collecting information from companies that sell anti-fraud and risk-scoring software, MeriTalk reported on Monday, November 23. The request for information was posted on November 17 by FEMA's Fraud Investigations and Inspections Division, working with the agency's procurement office. It describes a possible competitive test, with several vendors, of tools that identify potentially high-risk business entities, and says the capabilities could later apply to FEMA grant programs. FEMA lists nine kinds of technology it will consider: artificial intelligence, machine learning, predictive analytics, rules engines, entity resolution, anomaly detection, graph and network analytics, identity and business verification, and optical character recognition. It wants tools that work at scale and keep both false positives and false negatives low. According to OrangeSlices AI, a federal contracting news site that reviewed the same notice (number 70FA3127I00000002), FEMA also wants reviewable explanations for every alert, risk score and entity assessment. The request sets limits on the test itself. Per MeriTalk, vendors would not conduct live operational screening, and would not make grant eligibility determinations, adverse-action recommendations, or decisions affecting an individual's or entity's rights, benefits, eligibility or safety. Why it matters: The agency that pays out US disaster grants has put in writing, before any purchase, where an AI fraud tool must stop: it may score and flag businesses, and it may not decide. The request also names false positives as a cost to be limited, which matters in disaster aid because a wrong flag delays money to a legitimate recipient. This is early. It is market research for a technical trial, and the limits apply to the trial, with nothing said about whatever FEMA might deploy afterward. It follows the IRS pilot the show covered on November 14, in which an AI agent assembles a fraud case and hands it to an employee: two federal agencies in ten days describing the same division of labor between a model and a person. FEMA has not decided how it would buy such a tool or when work would start, OrangeSlices reports, no budget is stated, and a request for information commits the agency to nothing.

Sources: MeriTalk, OrangeSlices AI
