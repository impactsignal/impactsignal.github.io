The First Women Join EDITH, the World's Largest Trial of AI in Breast Cancer Screening: Nearly 700,000 Expected Across 30 UK Sites, With a Human Radiologist on Every Scan and an Opt-Out in the Invitation Letter

Women invited for routine NHS breast screening in Cambridgeshire, England, became the first participants in a national trial called EDITH on Monday, November 23, the NIHR Cambridge Biomedical Research Centre reported that day. The trial tests an AI-assisted tool for reading mammograms. It is funded with 10 million pounds from the National Institute for Health and Care Research, and its organizers describe it as the largest trial in the world of AI in breast cancer screening. Cambridgeshire is the first location, and the plan is 30 breast screening sites across the UK and nearly 700,000 women. The problem the trial addresses is reading time. Women aged 50 to 71 are invited for screening every three years, and about 2.1 million women in the UK are screened each year, which the Cambridge center says helps prevent around 1,300 deaths. Today each mammogram is reviewed by two specialist radiologists, and fewer than 1 percent of mammograms show signs of cancer, so most specialist time goes to images that turn out to be negative. In the trial the AI tool is used alongside the specialists. If it performs, many mammograms would need only one radiologist, which would free radiologists for diagnosis and for women who do have cancer. The announcement states the limits. Nothing changes during the screening appointment itself, only how the images may be reviewed afterward, and every mammogram will still be reviewed by at least one human radiologist. The letter sent to eligible women with their screening invitation describes the trial and explains how to opt out. The trial is led by Professor Fiona Gilbert of the University of Cambridge and Cambridge University Hospitals and Professor Sian Taylor-Phillips of the University of Warwick, with NHS England and the UK National Screening Committee. Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper visited the Breast Unit at Addenbrooke's Hospital for the opening and said AI "has huge potential, but we also need proper safeguards in place." Hits Radio reported the local reaction on November 24: Helen Saggars of the Peterborough Breast Cancer Support Group told the station there is a shortage of radiologists in Peterborough and that the wait for results "is still a nightmare for people." Why it matters: A national health system has started testing AI in a screening program at the scale of the program itself, as a trial with an opt-out, before deciding whether to adopt it. The design answers two questions that usually go unasked when AI enters screening: whether one radiologist plus the tool finds cancers as reliably as two radiologists, and whether women are told. The constraint it addresses is staffing, in a service that screens 2.1 million women a year with two readers per scan. No results exist yet. The UK National Screening Committee is a partner in the trial, and the trial's findings are what it will have in front of it when the question of national use comes up.

Sources: NIHR Cambridge Biomedical Research Centre, Hits Radio (Rayo)

India's Health Ministry Offers AI Tuberculosis X-Rays at a National Trade Fair and Reports 12,189 Stall Visits, but Not How Many People Were Scanned or Flagged; a Delhi Program That Published Its Numbers Confirmed 877 Cases From 18,377 AI Flags

India's Health Ministry pavilion at the India International Trade Fair in New Delhi recorded more than 12,189 cumulative stall visits between November 14 and 18, according to a ministry release carried by Medical Dialogues on November 22. The pavilion covers 989 square meters at Bharat Mandapam, with 39 stalls representing 24 national health programs, and the fair runs through November 27. One of those stalls offers visitors a tuberculosis screening with an AI-enabled handheld X-ray device. Tech Times read the releases on November 22 and reported what they leave out. The figure counts visits to stalls, so one person who stops at three stalls may be counted three times, and it covers only the business-visitor days, since public entry began November 19. It says nothing about the AI screening: no count of X-rays taken, no count of people flagged, no device maker or software vendor named, and no description of what happens to a visitor the software flags. That last gap matters because of how the software works. Each X-ray gets an abnormality score, and the program running it chooses the threshold above which a person is referred for testing. Tech Times set the pavilion against a Delhi program that did publish its results. Between July 2025 and March 2026, 3,566 community camps X-rayed 168,568 residents aged 15 and older. The software flagged 18,377 of them, 10.9 percent. Of those, 16,866 completed molecular testing of sputum samples, and 877 were confirmed to have TB: 5.2 percent of those tested and 0.52 percent of everyone screened. The pavilion's vendor is not named, so those results cannot be assumed to apply to it. For national context, Tech Times cites the World Health Organization's Global Tuberculosis Report 2026, released November 12: India's TB death rate fell 38 percent between 2015 and 2025, from 28 to 18 per 100,000 people, short of the 75 percent milestone set for 2025, and about 263,000 people in India died of TB in 2025. The ministry says its national campaign performed more than 110 million chest X-rays between December 2024 and November 2026. Why it matters: The Delhi numbers show what an AI chest X-ray screen produces in a real population: roughly one confirmed case for every 21 people flagged, and about 1,500 flagged people who never completed the confirming test. An AI flag is a referral, and its value depends on the threshold chosen and on whether the follow-up test happens. The ministry's fair release reports attendance and none of the three figures that would describe the screening (scanned, flagged, confirmed), so the public display of the technology comes with no evidence about how it is performing there. The fair stays open through November 27, and no screening count had been published as of the Tech Times report.

Sources: Tech Times, Medical Dialogues

SenseNet Raises $43 Million to Expand Wildfire Detection Across the US, Citing Early Alerts on More Than 10,000 Fires; the Count Is the Company's Own, and It Has Not Said How Much of the Money Is New Investment

SenseNet, a wildfire detection company based in Vancouver, British Columbia, announced a $43 million financing package on Tuesday, November 24, GeekWire reported that day. The company says the money will pay for expansion across the United States and internationally. The Series B round was led by Idealist Capital, with Fusion Fund, Stormbreaker Ventures, Thin Line Capital, National Bank of Canada and The Sitka Foundation taking part. GeekWire's reporter, Lisa Stiffler, spelled out what the headline number contains. The package combines newly issued shares, which put money into the company, secondary share sales, which pay existing shareholders, and debt, and SenseNet did not disclose how much of the $43 million falls into each category. According to PitchBook figures cited by GeekWire, the company had previously raised $12.78 million and has 30 employees. SenseNet combines ground sensors, AI-enabled cameras, satellite data, drones and predictive analytics. It says its monitoring covers 450 million acres in a dozen countries and that it has provided early alerts for more than 10,000 fires, in some cases hours before the first 911 call. Those are company statements. GeekWire names its competitors as OroraTech, Dryad and Pano AI, whose cameras are used by Washington state's Department of Natural Resources. A report published one day earlier shows the technology at the scale of one fire department. In a Rocky Mountain PBS piece carried by Daily Dispatch on November 23, Boulder Fire Rescue in Colorado described a system of six cameras watching rural Boulder County. The AI looks for visual anomalies that could be smoke, and a person verifies each one before an alert goes out. Boulder began working with Alert West, a detection company that grew out of research at the University of California San Diego, in 2022 after the Marshall Fire, the costliest wildfire in Colorado's history. The piece carries the headline "AI can't fight wildfires yet, but it's giving fire departments an edge in spotting them." Why it matters: The capital moving into AI wildfire detection is growing, and little independent measurement travels with it. A $43 million round for a 30-person company, with an undisclosed share going to existing shareholders and lenders, rests on an alert count that only the company has published. The Boulder system shows the same technology in operation: six cameras, a human check on every detection, and a department that presents it as a way to spot fires sooner. This is the fourth wildfire-detection item on the show in two weeks, and across them deployment and funding are both ahead of published data on how often the alerts are right and how much time they save. SenseNet has not disclosed the split between new shares, secondary sales and debt, so how much of the round reaches its stated US expansion is still unknown.

Sources: GeekWire, GovTech, Rocky Mountain PBS (via Daily Dispatch)

Kenyan Cancer Specialists Say AI Diagnosis Will Not Help Patients Whose Records Cannot Follow Them Between Hospitals: "If No System Can Connect the Data in Mombasa to Kisumu, Then AI Is Not Going to Be Very Useful"

Kenya's plans to use AI in cancer diagnosis could be undermined by fragmented patient records, weak referral systems and unequal access to specialists, doctors said at the 9th Kenya International Cancer Conference in Mombasa, Business Today Kenya reported on November 22. The country records an estimated 35,867 new cancer cases and nearly 23,000 deaths a year, according to Global Cancer Observatory figures cited in the report. The argument came from physician Professor Joseph Aluoch. He said AI can help specialists analyze pathology samples and catch abnormalities that are hard to see, and named cervical cancer screening as one place it could support earlier detection. Then he described the condition. "You may have your data in Mombasa; the next time you go to Kisumu, you will be seen by another doctor," he said. "If no system can connect the data in Mombasa to Kisumu, then AI is not going to be very useful." He added that AI is "not like a big computer that you bring from London and sit here," and that hospitals first have to produce and maintain accurate digital patient records. Doctors, he said, must remain responsible for clinical decisions. The report ties this to what happens after screening. The Kenya Society of Haematology and Oncology, working with Kilifi County, ran an outreach offering cervical, breast and prostate screening and set up referral routes to four hospitals, including Coast General Hospital and Mombasa Cancer Center. The society cited a 2016 Kenyan study of 1,094 women at three breast screening camps: 14 percent had abnormal clinical examinations and about 1.3 percent were diagnosed with invasive breast cancer, which means a screening result creates patients who need diagnosis and treatment somewhere. The society's president, Dr. Gladwell Kiarie, said AI is already improving diagnostic processes and multidisciplinary consultations, and that patients in rural and marginalized communities still face treatment costs and too few specialists. She called for stronger government policy and sustainable health financing. Why it matters: This is the constraint on medical AI stated by the clinicians who would use it. The limiting factors they name are the patient record, which today stays in the hospital that created it, and the referral path after a positive screen. Set next to today's lead, the contrast is specific: England can trial AI reading inside a screening program that already tracks every woman from invitation to result, while Kenya's specialists say the tracking has to be built before the AI has anything reliable to read. No new deployment or funding was announced at the conference. What is newly on record is the profession's own ordering of what comes first: accurate digital records and referral routes, then AI.

Sources: Business Today Kenya, Newstrends Kenya
