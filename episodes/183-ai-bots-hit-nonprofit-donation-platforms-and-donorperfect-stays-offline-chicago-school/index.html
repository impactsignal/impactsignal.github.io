#183: Impact Signals #183 — AI Bots Hit Nonprofit Donation Platforms and DonorPerfect Stays Offline, Chicago Schools Weigh Leaving Google Search, NHS England Deletes a 137-Trust AI Claim, Georgia's 53-Page AI Playbook

Top Stories

AI Bots Hit Several Nonprofit Fundraising Platforms at Once: DonorPerfect's Donor Database Is Still Offline and GoFundMe Pro Cuts Its Salesforce Link as a Precaution

The NonProfit Times reported on Thursday, November 12, that AI bots attacked multiple fundraising platforms in what technology experts described to the paper as a coordinated, multi-pronged assault on nonprofit applications. The on-the-record confirmation comes from Lauren Sheehan, president of SofterWare, which owns the donor management platform DonorPerfect: "We have a lot of evidence to show these attacks are being run and they're utilizing AI bots." She said the attack is contained, the root cause is identified, and the way in was the company's marketing website. A SofterWare official speaking on background described an AI agent that was "rapidly adapting its approaches and discovering new methods to attack systems within seconds or minutes." DonorPerfect's status page, updated at 00:23 Eastern on Friday, November 13, lists the DonorPerfect application and its API as unavailable, Classic Online Forms donation forms as restored and processing gifts, crowdfunding forms as still unavailable, and the SafeSave payments gateway as operational, with transfers into the database paused. GoFundMe spokesperson Emily Wright said GoFundMe Pro is not down, donation processing is uninterrupted, and its Salesforce integration "has been temporarily disabled as a precaution." Why it matters: The systems charities use to take and record donations were hit together, by one actor according to a named company president, in the second week of November, weeks before the year-end giving period. Nonprofits on these platforms can still take gifts online, and some cannot sync those gifts to the database where their donor records live. Two claims remain unconfirmed on the record: one source told the paper on background that funds were diverted from a platform in at least one case, and the paper says the bots' IP addresses are all outside the United States but could have been rerouted. SofterWare told clients it has no determination yet of who was affected or what, if anything, was taken. Sheehan gave no restoration timeline and suggested it could take several days, the company has published a security FAQ, and The NonProfit Times says it will update its report.

Sources: The NonProfit Times, DonorPerfect status page

Families Protest Outside Google Offices in Seven US Cities, and Chicago Public Schools Says It Will Look for Another Search Engine if Students Cannot Get Search Without AI

This follows a story the show covered on November 4, when Google said its AI tools would be off by default on K-12 accounts and then confirmed there would be no version of Search without AI. On Wednesday, November 11, families protested in seven cities, Ars Technica reported: Boston; Boulder, Colorado; Chicago; Durham, North Carolina; Los Angeles; New York City; and Portland, Oregon. In Chicago, dozens of protesters marched to Google's downtown office in cold rain, organized by Illinois Families for Public Schools, the group that in August pressed the district to pull out of a Gemini pilot covering every high school student. Chicago Public Schools, which already blocks ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini on student accounts, told the Chicago Sun-Times it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the AI mode, and added: "The District would like to continue to provide students with access to the Google search engine without the AI component. If such an arrangement can't be reached, the District will look to other search solutions." Why it matters: The dispute has moved from whether a chatbot is switched on to whether a district can buy the plain product at all. Ars Technica reports that the only way for a student to avoid AI in Search is to add "-ai" to every query, and the Sun-Times notes how hard a switch would be for a district that also relies on Google Classroom, Drive, Docs and Chromebooks. A Google spokesperson told the Sun-Times the company is updating AI search features for classrooms to give step-by-step guidance instead of quick answers, and did not answer questions about cutting off Search. Illinois Families for Public Schools wants a moratorium and a binding policy voted on by the school board; the district has guidelines first issued in 2024 and no binding policy. Google has said the change takes effect next year, Chicago has not decided its response, and New York and Los Angeles, which restrict student AI, face the same choice.

Sources: Chicago Sun-Times, Ars Technica

NHS England Deletes Its List of 137 Hospital Trusts "Realising Benefits" From an AI Data Platform After 29 of Them Say They Are Not Using It

NHS England has taken down a web page listing 137 hospital trusts as "live" with and "realising benefits" from the federated data platform, the AI-powered health data system built by Palantir, The Guardian reported on Thursday, November 12. The platform is meant to improve patient outcomes, make operating rooms more efficient and speed up discharges. The list came down after The Guardian reported that 29 of those trusts, about one in five, said the platform was either not live or that they were not using all or most of its applications. The evidence came from freedom of information requests that Medact, a health campaign group, sent to every hospital and integrated care board in England. A less detailed page now says early users are "already seeing real results," and NHS England did not respond to the paper's requests for comment. Why it matters: A national health service withdrew its own published adoption figure for an AI system after the hospitals on the list contradicted it, and the correction came from outside, through records requests sent hospital by hospital. It is the second claim about the platform to run into trouble: the Office for Statistics Regulation, the UK's statistics watchdog, previously told NHS England it had no evidence for a claim that the technology helped deliver more than 100,000 extra operations. Ministers are deciding whether to extend the £330 million contract to 2029. Ben Coleman, a Labour member of the House of Commons health and social care committee, told the paper, "When NHS England makes claims like this without checking them, it destroys trust." The Guardian says the regulator is now examining the claims about how widely the system is used.

Sources: The Guardian

Georgia Gives Cities and Counties With No AI Staff a 53-Page Playbook: Set the Rules First, Inventory the AI Already Inside Your Software, Require Human Approval Before an AI Agent Acts

The Georgia Technology Authority's Office of Artificial Intelligence released its Local Government AI Playbook on Thursday, November 12, StateScoop reported. The 53-page guide is written for cities and counties that lack dedicated AI teams, formal AI policies or the resources to write their own governance framework. Its author is Nikhil Deshpande, Georgia's chief digital and AI officer, and State Representative Dar'shun Kendrick asked the state to produce it, according to a post by state chief information officer Shawnzia Thomas. The playbook recommends that local governments first establish policies, build an inventory of the AI systems they already have, form an oversight committee and train employees, and it names human resources platforms, cybersecurity products, permitting systems and document management tools as places where AI features are already embedded. For agentic AI, meaning systems that take actions on their own, it recommends limited permissions at the start, human approval for consequential actions, detailed activity logs and a quick way to switch a system off. Why it matters: Most public-sector AI guidance has been written by and for large agencies with dedicated staff, while small counties and towns run permitting, benefits referrals and emergency services on software that now ships with AI built in. Georgia's document is aimed at the governments least able to write their own rules, covers AI agents as well as chat tools, and says AI should support public employees' work without replacing their professional judgment or existing staff. Thomas said it includes "adaptable templates" for governance, project evaluation and workforce readiness. The playbook is guidance with no mandate behind it, and whether Georgia's localities adopt it is not yet known.

Sources: StateScoop

Ghana's 2027 Budget Commits US$300 Million Over Two Years to a National AI Centre and to Upgrading Rural 2G Networks to 4G

Ghana's Finance Minister, Dr Cassiel Ato Forson, told Parliament on Thursday, November 12, that the government will commit US$300 million between 2027 and 2028 to establishing a National Artificial Intelligence Centre and upgrading rural 2G mobile networks to 4G, MyJoyOnline reported from the 2027 Budget Statement. The centre is described as shared infrastructure, with high-performance computing, secure data environments, applied research labs, model-testing facilities, training and start-up support, open to Ghanaian businesses, researchers, universities and public institutions that could not afford such resources on their own. Its stated priorities are agriculture, healthcare, energy, mining, logistics, Ghanaian languages and public services. Forson said moving rural networks from 2G to 4G would let farmers get weather forecasts and market information, receive digital payments and reach buyers, and would widen access to telehealth and educational resources in underserved communities. Why it matters: The budget attaches a dollar figure and a two-year window to public AI infrastructure in a West African country, and pairs it in the same line with the rural network upgrade that determines whether people outside the cities can reach AI services at all. Earlier this week the show covered a Ghanaian service built for SMS because 9 million Ghanaians do not use the internet; this budget addresses the same gap from the infrastructure side. The government says the centre will operate under safeguards covering data privacy, cybersecurity, accessibility and human accountability in high-impact decisions. The report does not say how the US$300 million divides between the centre and the network upgrade, and Parliament has yet to approve the 2027 budget.

Sources: MyJoyOnline

Abu Dhabi's Emergency Centre Says Its Goal Is for a Drone to Be the First Responder, and Describes Staff-Built AI Agents That Audit Hospitals' Continuity Plans

"Today the goal is for the drone to be the first responder to emergencies, crises and disasters in the Emirates," Shaikha Khamis Al Azeezi told Gulf News in an interview published on Tuesday, November 10. She directs the Business Continuity and Alternative Services Department at Abu Dhabi's Emergency, Crisis and Disaster Management Centre, and was speaking at an exhibition at the ADNEC Centre where the drone service is on show. In a large fire, she said, the drone follows the path of the flames and gives response teams information including safe evacuation routes; in severe weather it can show where water is collecting; and it carries cameras and sensors for search and rescue. The centre has also developed, with Abu Dhabi's Department of Health, a business continuity standard for the health sector, and an AI platform runs external audits of hospitals against it. A second system, Jaheziya, simulates exercises and incidents and scores each organization's readiness, and a third, AIN, is a set of AI agents that Al Azeezi said was "initially built by" the centre's own employees. Why it matters: Most coverage of AI in emergency response is about detection and dispatch. This account adds a less common use, checking whether hospitals are ready before anything happens, with agents built by a government body's own staff. The Gulf News headline says the centre "deploys" drones, while the official's own statement describes a goal, and the article gives no number of drones or of incidents flown. The evidence is one official's account in one interview, with no outcome data. Al Azeezi said she expects more automation of the emergency framework over the next five years.

Sources: Gulf News

New Zealand Researchers Are Building a National Landslide Model That Takes a Rain Forecast and Shows Where to Send Crews, and They Say Plainly It Will Not Predict a Specific Slope

Earth Sciences New Zealand and Massey University are leading a project called Sliding Lands Hōretireti Whenua that combines existing landslide databases with machine learning to estimate the probability of a landslide for a given amount of rain or seismic shaking, RNZ reported on November 11. Saskia de Vilder, an engineering geologist at Earth Sciences New Zealand, told RNZ's Nine to Noon that the goal is national-scale hazard models for the fast-moving landslides triggered by earthquakes and heavy rain: "These landslides are the ones where you're going to have limited or no evacuation time." The model divides the country into "landslide domains" based on climate, geology and rainfall. Because it is linked to rainfall, it can take a forecast ahead of a storm and produce maps that, in her words, "might be able to say help preposition where you might need response crews prior to an event." Why it matters: The model is being designed around a response decision, where to put crews before the rain arrives, and de Vilder set its limits herself: it will not give "site specific information" on where a landslide will happen, and it works as a screening tool that shows where authorities need to look more closely. The team is also building it to be inspected, so users can see where machine learning was used and which landslides informed a result, "so you can interrogate that model and see what's actually driving" it. RNZ's report gives no accuracy figures and no release date. The project is working with councils on how the output feeds their hazard mapping, which de Vilder described as ongoing conversations.

Sources: RNZ
